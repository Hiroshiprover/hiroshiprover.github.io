## Chapter 3: Electromagnetic Theory, Photons, and Light

### 3.1 Basic Definitions

#### Irradiance

**Irradiance** $I$ is the average energy transported by an electromagnetic wave per unit area per unit time. For a harmonic wave in vacuum, the magnitude of the **time-averaged Poynting vector** is the irradiance (unit: $\mathrm{W/m^2}$):

$$
I = \langle S \rangle_{T} = c^{2}\epsilon_{0}\,|\vec{E}_{0}\times \vec{B}_{0}|\;\langle \cos^{2}(\vec{k}\cdot \vec{r} -\omega t)\rangle
     = \frac{c^{2}\epsilon_{0}}{2}\,|\vec{E}_{0}\times \vec{B}_{0}|
$$

In vacuum, $|\vec{B}_{0}| = |\vec{E}_{0}|/c$, so the irradiance is proportional to the square of the electric field amplitude:

$$
I = \langle S \rangle_{T} = \frac{c\,\epsilon_{0}}{2}\,E_{0}^{2}
$$

The power $P$ (in watts) passing through an area $A$ is

$$
P = IA \qquad [\mathrm{W} = \mathrm{J/s}]
$$

---

#### Spherical Waves

A point source in a homogeneous medium radiates spherical waves. From the scalar wave equation:

$$
E(r,t) = \left(\frac{\mathcal{A}}{r}\right)\cos(kr \mp \omega t)
$$

where $\mathcal{A}$ is the source strength. At any distance $r$, $rE_0(r) = \text{const}$. Thus the amplitude decreases inversely with $r$, and the irradiance of a point source follows the inverse-square law:

$$
I \propto \frac{1}{r^{2}}.
$$

Note that spherical electromagnetic waves do not actually exist in reality. This is because Gauss's law $\nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \dfrac{\rho}{\epsilon}$ forcibly erases all differences in charge distribution — it is impossible to construct a charge distribution and motion that produces a spherical wave. Of course, it remains an excellent approximation.

---

#### Photon Flux

For a quasi-monochromatic beam of cross-sectional area $A$, the average photon flux (photons per second) is

$$
\Phi = \frac{AI}{h\nu_0}
$$

A single photon carries energy $E = h\nu$ ($h$ is Planck's constant). Numerically, a $P = 1$ mW He–Ne laser ($\lambda = 632.8\;\mathrm{nm}$) emits approximately $\Phi \approx 3.2 \times 10^{15}$ photons per second.

---

#### Radiation Pressure

Maxwell showed that electromagnetic radiation carries momentum. The instantaneous radiation pressure equals the energy density:

$$
P_r = u_E + u_B = \frac{S(t)}{c}
$$

The average radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface is

$$
\langle P_r \rangle = \frac{\langle S(t) \rangle}{c} = \frac{I}{c}
$$

Unit: $[P_r] = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} / \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}} \,(\text{pascals})$.

The force on an absorbing area $A$ is $F = AP_r$. Assuming the volume density of electromagnetic momentum is $p_v$, we have:

$$
F = \frac{p_v \cdot c\Delta t A}{\Delta t},
$$

Solving simultaneously yields:

$$
p_v = \frac{S}{c^2}
$$

In the photon picture, each quantum carries momentum

$$
p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \hbar k
$$

---

### 3.2 Light in Matter

In a medium with no free charges or currents, that is homogeneous, isotropic, and has linear constitutive relations, Maxwell's equations reduce to:

$$
\nabla^2 \vec{E} = \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial{\vec{E}}}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla^2 \vec{B} = \mu \epsilon \frac{\partial{\vec{B}}}{\partial t},
$$

These are classical wave equations, with traveling wave solutions:

$$
\vec{E}(\vec{r}, t) = \vec{E}_0 e^{i\left(\vec{k}\cdot \vec{r} - \omega t\right)}, \qquad \vec{B}(\vec{r}, t) = \frac{1}{v}\left(\hat{k} \times \vec{E}\right),
$$

Here we used the other two Maxwell equations to simplify. This requires electromagnetic waves to be transverse.

Define the phase velocity as the speed at which a surface of constant phase $kx-\omega t=\text{const}$ moves:

$$
v_p = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\omega}{k},
$$

Substituting the solution into Maxwell's equations proves that $v_p = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}$. In particular, the speed of light in vacuum is $c = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}$.

The (absolute) **refractive index** is defined as

$$
n \equiv \frac{c}{v_p} = \frac{c}{\omega}k = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{\epsilon_0\mu_0}}
$$

For non-magnetic materials ($\mu \approx \mu_0$), this simplifies to

$$
\boxed{n = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon_0}} = \sqrt{K_E}}
$$

where $K_E$ is the dielectric constant: $\epsilon = K_E \epsilon_0$.

The dielectric constant depends on the frequency of light: $K_E=K_E(\omega)$, so the phase velocity and refractive index are also frequency-dependent — this is called **dispersion**.

:::note[Origin of the Term "Dispersion"]

Why is it called "dispersion" (chromatic dispersion)? Imagine: for the same wave vector $k$, light of different frequencies has different phase velocities $v_p(\omega)$, and the dependence is positively correlated — lower-frequency light is slower, higher-frequency light is faster. And frequency corresponds to the color of light: low-frequency light is red, high-frequency light is violet. Thus, in a medium, different colors gradually separate over time. This is chromatic "dispersion." The concept has been generalized to many other fields, even though those fields involve no colors at all.

:::

:::note[Terminology Convention]

Hecht's book calls $\epsilon$ the "capacitivity" and $K_E$ the "dielectric constant" — a convention that differs from standard practice and is rather uninteresting.

:::

---

#### Group Velocity

Given the [dispersion relation](#5-light-in-matter--refractive-index-and-dispersion) for electromagnetic waves in a medium:

$$
\omega = v_p k = \frac{c}{n(\omega)}k,
$$

Differentiating with respect to $k$ yields:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}k} = \frac{c}{n(\omega)} - \frac{ck}{n^2(\omega)}\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}k} = \frac{1}{n(\omega)}\left(c - \omega\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}k}\right)
$$

Defining the **group velocity** $v_g = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d} k}$, we solve to obtain:

$$
v_g = \cfrac{c}{n(\omega) + \omega\cfrac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}\omega}}.
$$

This describes the propagation speed of a wave packet (its center), which can, under certain exotic dispersion relations, exceed the speed of light.

#### Lorentz Oscillator Model

In the classical picture, bound electrons respond to the driving electric field as damped harmonic oscillators. Let there be $N$ molecules per unit volume, each with $Z$ electrons, and each transition $j$ has a natural frequency $\omega_{0j}$ and oscillator strength $f_j$:

$$
n^{2}(\omega) = 1 + \frac{N q_e^{2}}{\epsilon_0 m_e} \sum_{j} \frac{f_j}{\omega_{0j}^{2} - \omega^{2}},
\qquad
\sum_j f_j = Z.
$$

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-2.png" alt="Refractive index dispersion relation" width="50%"/>
</div>

- **Normal dispersion**: far from resonance, $n$ increases with $\omega$.
- **Anomalous dispersion**: near the resonance frequency, $n$ decreases with increasing $\omega$, and strong absorption occurs here. The simple undamped model diverges at $\omega = \omega_{0j}$; actual treatment requires adding a damping term $\propto \gamma\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}/\mathrm{d}t$ to keep $n$ finite.

Slightly above the resonance frequency, the dielectric function can become negative (e.g., in metals below the plasma frequency, or in certain metamaterials). This leads to phenomena such as total external reflection and the possibility of negative refraction.

## Chapter 4: Propagation of Light

### 4.1 Reflection and Refraction

#### Huygens' Principle

**Huygens' Principle** states that every point on a wavefront can be regarded as a source of secondary spherical wavelets, and the new wavefront at any later time is the envelope of these wavelets.

Huygens' Principle can be used to prove the law of reflection and Snell's law.

#### Fermat's Principle

Let light propagate along a path $C$ in a (inhomogeneous) medium with refractive index $n(\vec{r})$. The optical path length is defined as the integral of the refractive index along the path:

$$
\text{OPL} = \int_C n(\vec{r}) \, \mathrm{d}s
$$

where $ds$ is the arc-length element of the path. Its physical meaning:

$$
n(\vec{r})\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{c}{v_p(\vec{r})}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{c\,\mathrm{d}t}{v_p(\vec{r})}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}t} = c\,\mathrm{d}t,
$$

That is, the optical path length is the distance light would travel in vacuum in the same amount of time.

**Fermat's Principle**: the actual path taken by light between two points $A$ and $B$ makes the optical path length stationary, i.e., the first-order variation of the optical path length is zero:

$$
\delta\int_A^B n\,\mathrm{d}s = 0
$$

Usually it is a minimum, but in some cases it can be a maximum or a saddle point. For example, light reflecting off a concave mirror may correspond to a maximum optical path; straight-line propagation in a homogeneous medium corresponds to a minimum optical path.

From Fermat's Principle one can derive the law of reflection, Snell's law, and the curved trajectories of light in inhomogeneous media.

#### Law of Reflection

Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection: $\theta_i = \theta_r$.

#### Snell's Law and Total Internal Reflection

At the interface between two media with refractive indices $n_i$ and $n_t$:

$$
n_i \sin\theta_i = n_t \sin\theta_t
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\frac{\sin\theta_i}{\sin\theta_t} = \frac{n_t}{n_i} \equiv n_{ti}
$$

where $n_{ti}$ is the relative refractive index of medium $t$ with respect to medium $i$.

When going from an optically denser to a rarer medium ($n_i > n_t$), Snell's law gives $\sin\theta_t = (n_i/n_t) \sin\theta_i$. If $\sin\theta_i$ exceeds $n_t/n_i$, no real $\theta_t$ exists, hence no refracted wave — the wave is totally internally reflected back into the denser medium.

The **critical angle** is defined as

$$
\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_t}{n_i} = n_{ti}
$$

At this angle $\theta_t = 90^{\circ}$, and the wave propagates exactly along the interface.

### 4.2 Fresnel Equations

Using electrodynamics, we can compute the amplitude of the transmitted and reflected waves, which depends on the polarization state. Let the wave be $\vec{E}_a = \vec{E}_{0a}e^{i\left(\vec{k}_a \cdot \vec{r} - \omega t\right)}$, where $a = i$ for the incident wave, $r$ for the reflected wave, and $t$ for the transmitted wave.

Electrodynamics guarantees that the frequency $\omega$ is the same for all three waves, and that the electric field completely determines the magnetic field: $\vec{B}(\vec{r}, t) = \dfrac{1}{v}\left(\vec{k} \times \vec{E}\right)$; choosing the origin at the point of incidence eliminates any extra phase for the reflected and transmitted waves.

Define:

$$
r_k = \left(\frac{E_{0r}}{E_{0i}}\right)_k, \qquad t_k = \left(\frac{E_{0t}}{E_{0i}}\right)_k
$$

where $k$ can be $\perp$, meaning the electric field is perpendicular to the plane of incidence; or $\parallel$, meaning the electric field is parallel to the plane of incidence. See the figures for reference.

1. TE polarization ($E$ perpendicular to the plane of incidence):

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-0.png" alt="TE" width="50%"/>
</div>

$$
r_{\perp} = \cfrac{\cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_i - \cfrac{n_t}{\mu_t} \cos\theta_t}{\cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_i + \cfrac{n_t}{\mu_t} \cos\theta_t}
\qquad
t_{\perp} = \cfrac{2\cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_i}{\cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_i + \cfrac{n_t}{\mu_t} \cos\theta_t}
$$

2. TM polarization ($E$ parallel to the plane of incidence):

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-1.png" alt="TM" width="50%"/>
</div>

$$
r_{\parallel} = \cfrac{\cfrac{n_t}{\mu_t} \cos\theta_i - \cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_t}{\cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_t + \cfrac{n_t}{\mu_t} \cos\theta_i}
\qquad
t_{\parallel} = \cfrac{2\cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_i}{\cfrac{n_t}{\mu_t} \cos\theta_i + \cfrac{n_i}{\mu_i} \cos\theta_t}
$$

:::caution[Sign Convention]

Note: the sign of $r$ depends on the choice of direction for the reflected $E$ field in the derivation. We follow the convention of Hecht's *Optics* (5th Edition), as shown in the figures.

:::


For non-magnetic media ($\mu_i\approx\mu_t\approx 1$), the **Fresnel equations** simplify to:

$$
r_{\perp} = \frac{n_i \cos\theta_i - n_t \cos\theta_t}{n_i \cos\theta_i + n_t \cos\theta_t}
\qquad
t_{\perp} = \frac{2n_i \cos\theta_i}{n_i \cos\theta_i + n_t \cos\theta_t}
$$
$$
r_{\parallel} = \frac{n_t \cos\theta_i - n_i \cos\theta_t}{n_i \cos\theta_t + n_t \cos\theta_i}
\qquad
t_{\parallel} = \frac{2n_i \cos\theta_i}{n_i \cos\theta_t + n_t \cos\theta_i}
$$

Using Snell's law, the above can be further simplified to:

$$
r_{\perp} = -\frac{\sin(\theta_i-\theta_t)}{\sin(\theta_i+\theta_t)}
\qquad
t_{\perp} = +\frac{2\sin\theta_t\cos\theta_i}{\sin(\theta_i+\theta_t)}
$$
$$
r_{\parallel} = +\frac{\tan(\theta_i-\theta_t)}{\tan(\theta_i+\theta_t)}
\qquad
t_{\parallel} = +\frac{2\sin\theta_t\cos\theta_i}{\sin(\theta_i+\theta_t)\cos(\theta_i-\theta_t)}
$$

We can define the reflectance $R$ and transmittance $T$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\perp} &= r_{\perp}^{2}, & T_{\perp} &= \left(\frac{n_t \cos\theta_t}{n_i \cos\theta_i}\right) t_{\perp}^{2} \\
R_{\parallel} &= r_{\parallel}^{2}, & T_{\parallel} &= \left(\frac{n_t \cos\theta_t}{n_i \cos\theta_i}\right) t_{\parallel}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

They represent power (irradiance) ratios. Under lossless conditions, $R+T=1$, i.e., energy conservation.

### 4.3 Evanescent Waves

Under total internal reflection, a transmitted wave actually still exists:

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-3.png" alt="Evanescent wave" width="50%"/>
</div>

The wave vector of the transmitted wave: $\vec{k}_t = (k_{tx}, k_{ty}, 0)$. From the geometry shown in the figure:

$$
k_{tx} = k_t \sin{\theta_t}, \qquad k_{ty} = k_t \cos{\theta_t},
$$

For total internal reflection, we cannot determine $\theta_t$, so we seek an equivalent formulation of Snell's law to express all quantities involving $\theta_t$ in terms of $\theta_i$:

$$
n_i \sin\theta_i = n_t \sin\theta_t \Longrightarrow n_i \cos\theta_i = n_t \cos\theta_t,
$$

That is:

$$
n_t\cos\theta_t = n_i\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta_t} = n_i\sqrt{1-\frac{\sin^2\theta_i}{n_{ti}^2}}
$$

Since $n = \dfrac{c}{v_p} = \dfrac{c}{\omega} k$, we have:

$$
k_y = k_t\cos\theta_t =  k_i\sqrt{1-\frac{\sin^2\theta_i}{n_{ti}^2}},
\qquad
k_x = k_t \frac{\sin\theta_i}{n_{ti}}
$$

The above discussion holds for arbitrary angles of incidence. Now consider the case where the incident angle exceeds the critical angle and total internal reflection occurs:

$$
\sin\theta_t = \frac{\sin\theta_i}{n_{ti}} \geq 1,
$$

yielding:

$$
k_{ty} = ik_t\sqrt{\frac{\sin^2\theta_i}{n_{ti}^2} - 1} \equiv i\beta
$$

where the attenuation coefficient is defined as:

$$
\beta = k_t \sqrt{\frac{\sin^{2}\theta_i}{n_{ti}^{2}} - 1}.
$$

$k_x$ remains unchanged. Thus, the transmitted wave can be written as:

$$
\vec{E}_t = \vec{E}_{0t}\exp\left[i\left(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r} - \omega t\right)\right] = \vec{E}_{0t} e^{-\beta y}\exp\left[i\left(k_t\frac{\sin\theta_i}{n_{ti}}x - \omega t\right)\right]
$$

That is, the amplitude decays exponentially with distance $y$ from the interface. The **penetration depth** is defined as $\delta = 1/\beta$, at which the amplitude has decayed to $1/e$ of its initial value. The penetration depth is typically on the order of one wavelength.

#### Frustrated Total Internal Reflection (FTIR)

If a denser medium is placed very close to the interface — so close that the evanescent wave still retains a significant amplitude — the evanescent field can couple energy across the gap, producing a propagating electromagnetic wave in the new denser medium. This effect is analogous to quantum tunneling, with transmission coefficient $T \propto e^{-2\beta d}$.

## Chapter 5: Geometrical Optics

### 5.1 Spherical Refracting Surfaces

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-5.png" alt="Spherical mirror" width="50%"/>
</div>

As shown, the optical path length is $\text{OPL} = n_1 l_o + n_2 l_i$. Using the law of cosines:

$$
l_o = \left(R^2 + (s_o+R)^2 - 2R(s_o+R)\cos\varphi \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}, \qquad l_i = \left(R^2 + (s_i-R)^2 + 2R(s_i-R)\cos\varphi \right)^{\frac{1}{2}},
$$

Fermat's principle gives:

$$
\frac{d}{d\varphi} \text{OPL} = 0 \Longrightarrow \frac{n_1(s_o+R)}{l_o} + \frac{n_2(s_i-R)}{l_i} = 0,
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\frac{n_1}{l_o} + \frac{n_2}{l_i} = \frac{1}{R}\left(\frac{n_2s_i}{l_i} - \frac{n_1s_o}{l_o}\right).
$$

In the small-angle limit, $s_i \approx l_i$, $s_o \approx l_o$, hence:

$$
\frac{n_1}{s_o} + \frac{n_2}{s_i} = \frac{n_2-n_1}{R},
$$

This is called the **paraxial approximation**, or **Gaussian approximation**.

If the incident light emerges parallel ($s_i=\infty$), define the first focal length (object-side focal length) as:
$$
f_o = \lim_{s_i \to \infty}s_o = \frac{n_1}{n_2-n_1}R
$$

Similarly, for parallel incident light ($s_o=\infty$), define the second focal length (image-side focal length) as:
$$
f_i = \lim_{s_o \to \infty}s_i = \frac{n_2}{n_2-n_1}R
$$

### 5.2 Thin Lenses

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-6.png" alt="Thin lens" width="50%"/>
</div>

A lens is the combination of two spherical refracting surfaces.

:::note[Sign Conventions]

Note: although the figure draws the image point $P'$ of the first surface on the left side of the lens, this does not affect the result — the form of the formulas remains unchanged. The difference is that here $s_{i1}$ is negative, representing a virtual image; when $P'$ falls on the right side (or even inside) the lens, $s_{i1}$ is positive (real image). Similarly, $R_2$ here should also be considered negative, since light is traveling from inside to outside.

:::

Consistent with the earlier discussion, under the paraxial approximation, for the first surface:

$$
\frac{n_m}{s_{o1}} + \frac{n_l}{s_{i1}} = \frac{n_l-n_m}{R_1},
$$

For the second surface:

$$
\frac{n_l}{s_{o2}} + \frac{n_m}{s_{i2}} = \frac{n_m-n_l}{R_2},
$$

Substituting the geometric relation $s_{o2} = d - s_{i1}$ and adding the two equations yields:

$$
\frac{n_m}{s_{o1}} + \frac{n_m}{s_{i2}} = (n_l-n_m)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right) + \frac{n_ld}{(s_{i1}-d)s_{i1}},
$$

In particular: for a thin lens ($d \to 0$) in air ($n_m \to 1$), we have:

$$
\frac{1}{s_{o}} + \frac{1}{s_{i}} = (n_l-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)
$$

This is known as the **Lensmaker's Formula**. When $d \to 0$, we naturally have $s_{o1} \equiv s_o$, and similarly for $s_i$.

Analogous to the earlier discussion, we define the focal length of a thin lens as:
$$
f_o = \lim_{s_i \to \infty}s_o, \qquad f_i = \lim_{s_o \to \infty}s_i,
$$

It is easy to see that for a thin lens the two are equal, so we drop the subscripts:
$$
f= \left[(n_l-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\right]^{-1},
$$

Thus the thin lens formula can also be written as:
$$
\frac{1}{s_o} + \frac{1}{s_i} = \frac{1}{f}.
$$

#### Magnification

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-7.png" alt="Magnification" width="50%"/>
</div>

:::info[Sign Convention]

Sign convention: distances above the optical axis are positive, below are negative. Hence: $y_o > 0$, $y_i < 0$.

:::

From the figure, by simple geometric relations we obtain:
$$
x_ox_i=f^2
$$

This is known as the Newtonian form of the thin lens formula. It is equivalent to the previous one.

:::info[Sign Convention]

Sign convention: $x_o$ is positive to the left of the focal point, and $x_i$ is positive to the right of the focal point.

:::

The **lateral magnification** of a lens is defined as:

$$
M_T \equiv \frac{y_i}{y_0} = -\frac{s_i}{s_o} = -\frac{x_i}{f} = -\frac{f}{x_o}.
$$

A positive/negative sign of the lateral magnification corresponds to an erect/inverted image; greater/less than $1$ corresponds to a magnified/diminished image. When and only when $s_o=s_i=2f$, $M_T=-1$, producing an inverted real image of equal size.

The **longitudinal magnification** of a lens is defined as:

$$
M_L \equiv \frac{\mathrm{d}x_i}{\mathrm{d}x_o},
$$

It is the ratio of an infinitesimal axial length in image space to the corresponding length in object space. For a single lens in a single medium, the longitudinal magnification is:

$$
M_L = -\frac{f^2}{x_o^2} = -M_T^2
$$

#### Summary of Image Types

| Lens Type | Object Position | Image Type | Image Position | Orientation | Relative Size |
|---------|---------|--------|--------|------|---------|
| Converging | $\infty > s_o > 2f$ | Real | $f < s_i < 2f$ | Inverted | Diminished |
| Converging | $s_o = 2f$ | Real | $s_i = 2f$ | Inverted | Equal |
| Converging | $f < s_o < 2f$ | Real | $s_i > 2f$ | Inverted | Magnified |
| Converging | $s_o = f$ | — | $\pm\infty$ | — | — |
| Converging | $s_o < f$ | Virtual | $\|s_i\| > s_o$ | Erect | Magnified |
| Diverging | Any position | Virtual | $\|s_i\| < \|f\|$ | Erect | Diminished |

#### Thin Lens Combinations

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-8.png" alt="Thin lens combination" width="70%"/>
</div>

:::note[Note]

Although the figure depicts two converging lenses with separation $d$ smaller than either focal length $f_i$, the following derivation holds for arbitrary lenses and arbitrary separations. The image formed by the second lens is not drawn here.

:::

For $L_1$ we have:

$$
\frac{1}{s_{i1}} = \frac{1}{f_1} - \frac{1}{s_{o1}} \Longrightarrow s_{i1} = \frac{f_1s_{o1}}{s_{o1}-f_1},
$$

Similarly for $L_2$:

$$
s_{i2} = \frac{f_2s_{o2}}{s_{o2}-f_2}
$$

And from the geometric relation $s_{o2} = d - s_{i1}$, substituting yields:

$$
s_{i2} = \frac{f_2d - f_2s_{o1}f_1 / (s_{o1} - f_1) }{d - f_2 - s_{o1} f_1 /(s_{o1} - f_1)}.
$$

The lateral magnification of a compound lens is always the product of the two individual magnifications:

$$
M_T = M_{T1}M_{T2} = \frac{f_1s_{i2}}{d(s_{o1}-f_1)-s_{o1}f_1}.
$$

We can similarly define the front and back focal lengths of the lens combination:

$$
\text{front focal length} = \lim_{s_{i2} \to \infty} s_{o1} = \frac{f_1(d-f_2)}{d-(f_1+f_2)}, \qquad \text{back focal length} = \lim_{s_{o1} \to \infty} s_{i2} = \frac{f_2(d-f_1)}{d-(f_1+f_2)},
$$

When the lens separation $d \to 0$, the front and back focal lengths become equal, giving:

$$
f = \frac{f_1f_2}{f_1+f_2}.
$$

If $N$ lenses are in intimate contact, the equivalent focal length is:

$$
\frac{1}{f} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{f_i}.
$$

#### Spherical Mirrors*

:::note

Not a key topic for 2026, but very simple, so included here for completeness.

:::

Under the paraxial approximation, a spherical mirror approximates a parabolic mirror. The imaging formula is:

$$
\frac{1}{s_o} + \frac{1}{s_i} = -\frac{2}{R},
$$

Focal length:

$$
f = -\frac{R}{2}.
$$

Its imaging properties are almost identical to those of lenses: a concave mirror corresponds to a converging lens, and a convex mirror to a diverging lens.

### 5.3 Fiber Optics

For an optical fiber of length $L$, core refractive index $n_f$, cladding refractive index $n_c$, and external medium air ($n_a \approx 1$):

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-9.png" alt="Optical fiber" width="50%"/>
</div>

The geometric path length is

$$
l = \frac{L}{\cos\theta_t} = n_f L\,(n_f^{2} - \sin^{2}\theta_i)^{-1/2}
$$

Inside a fiber of diameter $D$, the number of reflections is

$$
N = \frac{l}{D/\sin\theta_t} \pm 1= \frac{L\sin\theta_i}{D\,(n_f^{2} - \sin^{2}\theta_i)^{1/2}} \pm 1
$$

$\pm 1$ indicates rounding to the nearest integer. Example: for $D = 50\;\mu\mathrm{m}$, $n_f = 1.6$, $\theta_i = 30^{\circ}$, there are about 2000 reflections per foot ($\sim 6600$ reflections/m).

#### Intermodal Dispersion

In a multimode fiber, different rays (modes) travel different path lengths. The fastest is the ray at normal incidence:

$$
t_{\min} = \frac{L}{v_f} = \frac{Ln_f}{c}
$$

The slowest is the ray incident at the critical angle for total internal reflection. From geometry:

$$
\cos\theta_t = \sin\theta_c = \frac{n_c}{n_f},
$$

where $n_c$ is the refractive index of the fiber cladding. Hence:

$$
t_{\max} = \frac{l}{v_f} = \frac{Ln_f}{c\cos\theta_t} = \frac{Ln_f^2}{cn_c},
$$

This leads to pulse broadening:

$$
\Delta t = t_{\max} - t_{\min} = \frac{L n_f}{c}\left(\frac{n_f}{n_c} - 1\right)
$$

When the broadening becomes large enough, signals become indistinguishable, limiting the length of a single fiber.

### 6.1 Thick Lenses

A thick lens can be treated as two spherical refracting surfaces separated by a distance $d$ at their vertices, or as a combination of two thin lenses. As in the derivation of the thin lens formula earlier, see the figure:

![Thick lens](/img/optics/image-10.png)

The focal length can be derived as:

$$
\frac{1}{f} = (n_l-1)\left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{(n_l-1)d_l}{n_lR_1R_2} \right), 
$$

Position of the principal planes (location of the equivalent thin lens):

$$
h_1 = -\frac{f(n_l-1)d_l}{R_2n_l}, \qquad h_2 = -\frac{f(n_l-1)d_l}{R_1n_l}
$$

The refraction of a thick lens in air can be written as:

$$
\frac{1}{s_o} + \frac{1}{s_i} = \frac{1}{f},
$$

Consistent with before, provided $s_o, s_i$ are measured from the two principal planes. Likewise, the Newtonian form of the lens formula also holds:

$$
x_ox_i=f^2,
$$

And the lateral magnification:

$$
M_T \equiv \frac{y_i}{y_0} = -\frac{s_i}{s_o} = -\frac{x_i}{f} = -\frac{f}{x_o}.
$$

### 6.2 Matrix Methods

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-12.png" alt="Thick lens matrix method" width="50%"/>
</div>

This figure is severely distorted: under the paraxial approximation, all angles are extremely small, so we directly use the approximation $\theta \approx \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta$.
Furthermore, point $C$ is the center of curvature of the first lens surface, which is also severely distorted.

As shown: applying Snell's law at $P_1$, noting that $\alpha_1 = \dfrac{y_1}{R_1}$:

$$
n_{i1}\theta_{i1} = n_{t1}\theta_{t1} \Longrightarrow n_{t1}\alpha_{t1} = n_{i1}\alpha_{i1} - \left(\frac{n_{t1}-n_{i1}}{R_1}\right) y_1
$$

Define the power of a single refracting surface as:

$$
\mathscr{D}_1 = \frac{n_{t1}-n_{i1}}{R_1},
$$

Thus the refraction equation at the first interface:

$$
n_{t1}\alpha_{t1} = n_{i1}\alpha_{i1} - \mathscr{D}_1 y_1,
$$

It then traverses the lens of uniform refractive index to reach $P_2$, with the transfer equation:

$$
y_2 = y_1 + d_{21} \alpha_{t1}.
$$

#### Refraction Matrix

The refraction matrix describes the process of a ray entering/exiting a lens.

Rewrite the refraction equation:

$$
n_{t1}\alpha_{t1} = n_{i1}\alpha_{i1} - \mathscr{D}_1 y_{i1},
$$

And add the trivial term:

$$
y_{t1} = 0 + y_{i1},
$$

That is: the ray before refraction and the ray after refraction have the same height at $P_1$. Thus the process of a ray entering/exiting a lens can be described by a matrix equation:

$$
\iota_{t1} = \mathscr{R}_1\iota_{i1},
$$

where:

$$
\iota_k \equiv \begin{pmatrix}
n_k\alpha_k \\
y_k
\end{pmatrix}, \, k=t1, i1, \qquad
\mathscr{R}_1 \equiv \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -\mathscr{D}_1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -(n_{t1}-n_{i1})/R_1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

When entering the lens, $n_{t1}$ is the refractive index of the medium after refraction, i.e., the lens refractive index; $n_{i1}$ is the refractive index before refraction, i.e., the external medium (usually air). When exiting the lens, $n_{t_2} = n_{i3}$, the external medium refractive index, usually equal to $n_{i1}$ and typically air $n_a \approx 1$; $n_{i2}=n_{t1}$ is the lens refractive index.

:::

#### Transfer Matrix

The transfer matrix describes the propagation of a ray within a medium/lens.

Trivial term:

$$
n_{i2}\alpha_{i2} = n_{t1}\alpha_{t1} + 0
$$

That is, at points $P_1$ and $P_2$ the refractive index is the same and the angle is the same.

Rewrite the transfer equation:

$$
y_{i2} = \frac{d_{21}}{n_{t1}} n_{t1} \alpha_{t1} + y_{t1},
$$

Thus the transfer of a ray inside the lens can be written as a matrix equation:

$$
\iota_{i2} = \mathscr{T}_{21} \iota_{t1}
$$

where:
$$
\mathscr{T}_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ d_{21}/n & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ d_1/n & 1 \end{pmatrix} 
$$

The second equality means that under the paraxial approximation the horizontal distance $P_1P_2$ approximately equals the lens thickness $d_1$.

The transfer matrix can also describe ray propagation in air, simply by taking $n=1$. When two lenses are separated by $d$, light exiting the first lens must be multiplied by the air transfer matrix before reaching the second lens.

#### System Matrix

For a thick lens, light enters the lens, transfers inside, then exits, so the overall effect of the lens on the ray can be expressed as:

$$
\iota_{t2} = \mathscr{R}_2\,\mathscr{T}_{21}\,\mathscr{R}_1\,\iota_{i1}
                     = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix} \iota_{i1}
$$

where, for a single lens:

$$
\mathscr{A} = 
\begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
1-\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_2d_l}{n_l} & -\mathscr{D}_1-\mathscr{D}_2+\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_2\mathscr{D}_1d_l}{n_l} \\
\dfrac{d_l}{n_l} & 1-\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_1d_l}{n_l}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Note that in air:

$$
-a_{12} = \mathscr{D}_1+\mathscr{D}_2-\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_2\mathscr{D}_1d_l}{n_l} = (n_l-1)\left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{(n_l-1)d_l}{n_lR_1R_2} \right),
$$

This is exactly the equivalent focal length of the thick lens. Thus we can similarly define the equivalent power: $\mathscr{D}_l = -a_{12}$.

#### Computing Thick Lens Formulas Using Matrix Methods

<div align="center">
<img src="/img/optics/image-13.png" alt="Principal planes" width="50%"/>
</div>

For a ray incident parallel to the optical axis ($\alpha_{i1} = 0$) at height $y_{i1}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} n_{t2}\alpha_{t2} \\ y_{t2} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ y_{i1} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} a_{12} y_{i1} \\ a_{22} y_{i1} \end{bmatrix}
$$

From geometry:

$$
f_2 = \frac{y_{i1}}{-\alpha_{t2}} = -\frac{n_{t2}y_{i1}}{n_{t2}\alpha_{t2}} = -\frac{n_{t2}}{a_{12}}
$$

where the angle $\alpha_{t2}$ is taken as negative because the ray propagates downward (negative slope). Thus the distance from the rear vertex $V_2$ to $H_2$ is:

$$
H_2V_2 = \frac{y_{i1} - y_{t2}}{\alpha_{t2}} = -\frac{y_{i1} - a_{22}y_{i1}}{a_{12}y_{i1}/n_{t2}}
      = -\frac{f\,(n_l - 1)\,d_l}{n_l R_1}
$$

The first focal length is similar:

$$
f_1 = H_1F_o = -\frac{n_{i1}}{a_{12}}
\qquad
H_1V_1 = \frac{(a_{11}-1)n_{i1}}{a_{12}} = -\frac{f\,(n_l - 1)\,d_l}{n_l R_2}
$$

#### Matrix Description of Thin Lenses

Setting $d_l=0$ gives the matrix description of a thin lens:

$$
\mathscr{A} = \mathscr{R_2}\mathscr{T}\mathscr{R_1} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -(\mathscr{D}_1 + \mathscr{D}_2) \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where:

$$
\mathscr{D}_1 + \mathscr{D}_2 = \frac{n_l-1}{R_1} - \frac{n_l-1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{f},
$$

Just as for the thick lens earlier. Thus:

$$
\mathscr{A} =  \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1/f \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

In particular, consider the combination of two thin lenses. The total system matrix is:

$$
\mathscr{A} = \mathscr{A_2}\mathscr{T}\mathscr{A_1} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1/f_2 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
d & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1/f_1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
1-\dfrac{d}{f_2} & -\dfrac{1}{f_2}-\dfrac{1}{f_2}+\dfrac{d}{f_1f_2} \\
d & 1-\dfrac{d}{f_1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Total focal length:

$$
\frac{1}{f} = -a_{12} = \frac{1}{f_2}+\frac{1}{f_2}-\frac{d}{f_1f_2}.
$$

This also allows a clearer view of the earlier thick-lens system matrix:

$$
\mathscr{A} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
1-\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_2d_l}{n_l} & -\mathscr{D}_1-\mathscr{D}_2+\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_2\mathscr{D}_1d_l}{n_l} \\
\dfrac{d_l}{n_l} & 1-\dfrac{\mathscr{D}_1d_l}{n_l}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

Note that $\mathscr{D}_i = 1/f_i$. Thus a thick lens is nothing but two thin lenses combined using a transfer matrix with refractive index $n_l$.

### 6.3 Spherical Aberration

:::note

Not a key topic for 2026, but it appeared in the homework and I was worried it might be on the exam, so I compiled it here.

:::

[Recall](#51-spherical-refracting-surfaces):

$$
l_o = \left(R^2 + (s_o+R)^2 - 2R(s_o+R)\cos\varphi \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}, \qquad l_i = \left(R^2 + (s_i-R)^2 + 2R(s_i-R)\cos\varphi \right)^{\frac{1}{2}},
$$

and:

$$
\frac{n_1}{l_o} + \frac{n_2}{l_i} = \frac{1}{R}\left(\frac{n_2s_i}{l_i} - \frac{n_1s_o}{l_o}\right),
$$

The above expressions are exact. Previously we discussed only the paraxial approximation: $\varphi \ll 1$, where $s_o\approx l_o, s_i \approx l_i$. But when this condition does not hold, we need to apply higher-order corrections, known as spherical aberration. Taylor-expanding the exact expression to order $h^{2}$ gives the third-order correction, where $h$ is the incidence height of the ray on the spherical surface; note from geometry: $h = R\sin\varphi$.

:::note[Question]

From this perspective, it's unclear why this correction is called a third-order correction. Perhaps expanding directly in $\varphi$ to third order, rather than changing variables to $h$, would yield the same result?

:::

First rewrite $l_o, l_i$ using $1-\cos\varphi = 2\sin^{2}(\varphi/2)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
l_o^{2} &= R^{2} + (s_o+R)^{2} - 2R(s_o+R)\cos\varphi \\
        &= s_o^{2} + 2R(s_o+R) - 2R(s_o+R)\cos\varphi \\
        &= s_o^{2} + 4R(s_o+R)\sin^{2}\frac{\varphi}{2},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
l_i^{2} &= R^{2} + (s_i-R)^{2} + 2R(s_i-R)\cos\varphi \\
        &= s_i^{2} + 2R(s_i-R)\cos\varphi - 2R(s_i-R) \\
        &= s_i^{2} - 4R(s_i-R)\sin^{2}\frac{\varphi}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now expand. For rays near the paraxial region, $\varphi \ll 1$, so

$$
\sin\frac{\varphi}{2} \approx \frac{\varphi}{2} \approx \frac{h}{2R},
\qquad
\sin^{2}\frac{\varphi}{2} \approx \frac{h^{2}}{4R^{2}}.
$$

Substituting gives:

$$
l_o^{2} \approx s_o^{2} + \frac{(s_o+R)h^{2}}{R},
\qquad
l_i^{2} \approx s_i^{2} - \frac{(s_i-R)h^{2}}{R}.
$$

Take the square root and expand to order $h^{2}$ (using $\sqrt{1+x}\approx 1 + x/2$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
l_o &\approx s_o\left(1 + \frac{(s_o+R)h^{2}}{2R s_o^{2}}\right) = s_o + \frac{(s_o+R)h^{2}}{2R s_o}, \\[6pt]
l_i &\approx s_i\left(1 - \frac{(s_i-R)h^{2}}{2R s_i^{2}}\right) = s_i - \frac{(s_i-R)h^{2}}{2R s_i}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Furthermore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{l_o} &\approx \frac{1}{s_o}\left(1 - \frac{(s_o+R)h^{2}}{2R s_o^{2}}\right) = \frac{1}{s_o} - \frac{(s_o+R)h^{2}}{2R s_o^{3}}, \\[6pt]
\frac{1}{l_i} &\approx \frac{1}{s_i}\left(1 + \frac{(s_i-R)h^{2}}{2R s_i^{2}}\right) = \frac{1}{s_i} + \frac{(s_i-R)h^{2}}{2R s_i^{3}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now transform the exact relation:

$$
\frac{n_1}{l_o} + \frac{n_2}{l_i} = \frac{1}{R}\left(\frac{n_2s_i}{l_i} - \frac{n_1s_o}{l_o}\right) \;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{n_1(R+s_o)}{l_o} = \frac{n_2(s_i-R)}{l_i}.
$$

Substitute the expansions of $1/l_o$, $1/l_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_1(R+s_o)\left[\frac{1}{s_o} - \frac{(s_o+R)h^{2}}{2R s_o^{3}}\right]
= n_2(s_i-R)\left[\frac{1}{s_i} + \frac{(s_i-R)h^{2}}{2R s_i^{3}}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Expand the left and right sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{LHS} &= \frac{n_1R}{s_o} + n_1 - \frac{n_1(R+s_o)^{2} h^{2}}{2R s_o^{3}}, \\[4pt]
\text{RHS} &= n_2 - \frac{n_2R}{s_i} + \frac{n_2(s_i-R)^{2} h^{2}}{2R s_i^{3}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearrange, separating the zeroth-order terms from the $h^{2}$-order terms:

$$
\frac{n_1R}{s_o} + \frac{n_2R}{s_i} = n_2 - n_1 + \frac{n_1(R+s_o)^{2} h^{2}}{2R s_o^{3}} + \frac{n_2(s_i-R)^{2} h^{2}}{2R s_i^{3}}.
$$

Divide both sides by $R$:

$$
\frac{n_1}{s_o} + \frac{n_2}{s_i} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R} + \frac{n_1(R+s_o)^{2} h^{2}}{2R^{2} s_o^{3}} + \frac{n_2(s_i-R)^{2} h^{2}}{2R^{2} s_i^{3}}.
$$

Simplify the coefficients of the $h^{2}$ terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(R+s_o)^{2}}{2R^{2}s_o^{3}}
&= \frac{R^{2} + 2Rs_o + s_o^{2}}{2R^{2}s_o^{3}}
 = \frac{1}{2s_o^{3}} + \frac{1}{R s_o^{2}} + \frac{1}{2R^{2}s_o}
 = \frac{1}{2s_o}\left(\frac{1}{s_o} + \frac{1}{R}\right)^{2}, \\[6pt]
\frac{(s_i-R)^{2}}{2R^{2}s_i^{3}}
&= \frac{s_i^{2} - 2Rs_i + R^{2}}{2R^{2}s_i^{3}}
 = \frac{1}{2R^{2}s_i} - \frac{1}{R s_i^{2}} + \frac{1}{2s_i^{3}}
 = \frac{1}{2s_i}\left(\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{s_i}\right)^{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting yields the third-order spherical aberration correction formula:

$$
\frac{n_1}{s_o} + \frac{n_2}{s_i} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}
+ h^{2}\left[ \frac{n_1}{2s_o}\left(\frac{1}{s_o} + \frac{1}{R}\right)^{2}
                + \frac{n_2}{2s_i}\left(\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{s_i}\right)^{2} \right].
$$

For a converging lens, the spherical aberration correction is negative, so marginal rays converge earlier and fall before the focal point; for a diverging lens the opposite occurs.

## Chapter 7: Superposition of Waves

### 7.1 Fourier Series and Fourier Transform

#### Continuous Fourier Transform

Any (absolutely integrable) function $f(x)$ can be decomposed into a continuous superposition of sinusoidal waves of different frequencies:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(k)\, e^{ikx}\,\mathrm{d}k,
\qquad
F(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, e^{-ikx}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

$F(k)$ is called the Fourier transform of $f(x)$, and $f(x)$ is the inverse transform.

#### Discrete Fourier Transform and Fourier Series

For periodic functions (period $T$), $F(k)$ manifests as Dirac peaks, so the inverse transform can be directly decomposed into a sum — that is, the function can be expanded into a Fourier series:

$$
f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, e^{i n\omega_0 t},
\qquad
c_n = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} f(t)\, e^{-i n\omega_0 t}\,\mathrm{d}t,
$$

where $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$.

### 7.2 Coherence

Monochromatic light does not exist in nature. In any case, the light emitted by a source always consists of wave packets of similar frequencies, emitted over a finite duration.

#### Frequency Bandwidth

A wave train of finite duration is not strictly monochromatic. From Fourier analysis: a wave packet of duration $\Delta t$ necessarily has frequency uncertainty $\Delta\nu$, satisfying $\Delta\nu\,\Delta t \sim 1$. The longer the wave train (larger $\Delta t$), the better the monochromaticity (smaller $\Delta\nu$).

#### Temporal Coherence

In real atoms, electronic transitions have finite durations $\Delta t \sim 10^{-8}$–$10^{-9}$ s, so the emitted wave trains are finite. Other factors also contribute, such as random thermal motion or collisions. This produces a finite frequency broadening:

$$
\Delta \nu \sim \frac{1}{\Delta t}
$$

Definition: the **coherence time** $\Delta t_c$ is roughly the time interval over which the wave maintains a predictable phase. The **coherence length** is

$$
\Delta x_c = c\,\Delta t_c
$$

It reflects the degree to which the wave maintains good sinusoidal character in space, allowing its phase to be well predicted.

## Chapter 8: Polarization

### 8.1 Basic Types of Polarization

Let a monochromatic plane wave propagate along the $z$ direction. The electric field can be decomposed into two orthogonal components:

$$
E_x(z,t) = E_{0x}\cos(kz - \omega t), \qquad
E_y(z,t) = E_{0y}\cos(kz - \omega t + \varepsilon),
$$

where $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_y - \varepsilon_x$ is the phase difference of the $y$ component relative to the $x$ component. The polarization state is completely determined by the three parameters $E_{0x}, E_{0y}, \varepsilon$.

- **Linear polarization**: $\varepsilon = 0$ or $\pm\pi$ (the two components are in phase or out of phase). Then $E_y/E_x = \pm E_{0y}/E_{0x}$ is constant, and the electric field oscillates along a fixed line.

- **Circular polarization**: $E_{0x} = E_{0y} = E_0$ and $\varepsilon = \pm \pi/2$. The electric field rotates on a circle of radius $E_0$. $\varepsilon = +\pi/2$ ($y$ leads $x$) corresponds to right-handed, $\varepsilon = -\pi/2$ to left-handed.

:::info[Convention]

The definition of right-handed/left-handed depends on the viewing direction; the convention is to look against the direction of light propagation.

:::

- **Elliptical polarization**: $E_{0x} \neq E_{0y}$ or $\varepsilon \neq 0, \pm \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \pm \pi$. The trajectory of the electric field is an ellipse.

The complex description is more concise: $\tilde{E}_x = E_{0x}e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$, $\tilde{E}_y = E_{0y}e^{i(kz-\omega t+\varepsilon)}$, then:

$$
\frac{\tilde{E}_y}{\tilde{E}_x} = \frac{E_{0y}}{E_{0x}}\,e^{i\varepsilon}
$$

- Linear polarization: $e^{i\varepsilon} = \pm 1$, ratio is real;
- Circular polarization: $E_{0x}=E_{0y}$ and $e^{i\varepsilon} = \pm i$, ratio is purely imaginary;
- Elliptical polarization: arbitrary complex number. This naturally leads to the Jones vector representation discussed later.

### 8.2 Malus's Law

**Malus's Law**: a linear polarizer transmits only the electric field component parallel to its transmission axis. For incident linearly polarized light of irradiance $I(0)$ with polarization direction at angle $\theta$ to the transmission axis:

$$
I(\theta) = I(0) \cos^{2}\theta, \qquad I(0) = \frac{c\epsilon_0}{2}E_{01}^2
$$

### 8.3 Birefringence

In anisotropic crystals, the dielectric constant (and hence the refractive index) depends on the polarization direction relative to the crystal axes. The Lorentz oscillator model extended to anisotropic media gives
<img align="right" src="/img/optics/image-15.png" alt="Birefringence" width="30%"/>

$$
n^{2}(\omega) = 1 + \frac{N q_e^{2}}{\epsilon_0 m_e}\left(\frac{1}{\omega_0^{2} - \omega^{2}}\right)
$$

where the resonance frequency $\omega_0$ differs for different crystal directions. This means that for light of the same frequency, different polarization directions experience different refractive indices, as shown in the figure on the right.

#### Uniaxial Crystals

The most important type of birefringent crystal is the uniaxial crystal (e.g., calcite CaCO$_3$), which has a special direction called the optic axis; light propagating along this direction experiences no birefringence. In other words, a uniaxial crystal has rotational symmetry about the optic axis.

When a beam of unpolarized light is incident on a uniaxial crystal, it splits into two beams with mutually orthogonal polarizations:

- **Ordinary ray (o-ray)**: polarization direction perpendicular to the plane formed by the optic axis and the propagation direction (principal section). The o-ray obeys Snell's law, and its refractive index $n_o$ is independent of the propagation direction — it is constant in all directions.

- **Extraordinary ray (e-ray)**: polarization direction parallel to the principal section. The e-ray generally does not obey Snell's law because the refractive index $n=n(\theta)$ depends on the exit angle $\theta$. When light propagates along the optic axis, $n = n_o$; when propagating perpendicular to the optic axis, $n$ takes an extreme value (denoted $n_e$). $|n_e - n_o|$ is called the birefringence, characterizing the degree of anisotropy of the crystal.

Near absorption bands, the o-ray and e-ray have different absorption coefficients, so the color appears different when viewed along different directions — the material is said to exhibit dichroism.

#### Optic Axis Parallel to the Surface

Consider the case where the optic axis lies in the plane of the crystal surface, with light incident normal to the surface. Then the o-ray and e-ray propagate in the same direction (no deflection), but they experience different refractive indices $n_o$ and $n_e$, and thus travel at different speeds.

Let the crystal thickness be $d$, take the optic axis direction as $\hat{i}$ (x-axis), and the perpendicular direction as $\hat{j}$ (y-axis). The incident light is linearly polarized at an arbitrary angle to the x-axis:

$$
\vec{E}(z, t) = \hat{i}E_{0x}e^{i(k_xz-\omega t)} + \hat{j}E_{0y}e^{i(k_yz-\omega t)},
$$

Inside the crystal, the wave numbers $k$ in the two directions change:

$$
k_i = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_i} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}n_i, \, i=o, e, \qquad \lambda_0 = \frac{2\pi c}{\omega}
$$

Thus after traveling a distance $d$, the phase difference between the two components is:

$$
\Delta\phi = k_e d - k_o d = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\,d\,(n_e - n_o),
$$

Therefore, by controlling the thickness $d$, one can precisely adjust the phase difference $\Delta\phi$ — this is precisely the working principle of wave plates:

#### Wave Plates

**Half-wave plate ($\Delta\phi = \pi$):** the exiting o and e components have a phase difference of $\lambda/2$. It rotates the polarization direction of linearly polarized light by $2\theta$ ($\theta$ is the angle between the input polarization direction and the fast axis), and reverses the handedness of circular/elliptical polarization. Thickness:

$$
d\,|n_o - n_e| = (2m+1)\frac{\lambda_0}{2}, \qquad m = 0,1,2,\dots
$$

**Quarter-wave plate ($\Delta\phi = \pi/2$):** converts linearly polarized light into circularly (or elliptically) polarized light, and vice versa. When the input linear polarization makes $45^{\circ}$ with either principal axis, the output is circularly polarized light. Thickness:

$$
d\,|n_o - n_e| = (4m+1)\frac{\lambda_0}{4}, \qquad m = 0,1,2,\dots
$$

Note: the effect of a wave plate depends on the angle between the polarization direction of the light and its fast axis (the direction of smaller refractive index). For example, for a quarter-wave plate, light polarized parallel to its fast axis is unaffected, while light polarized at $45^\circ$ to it becomes circularly polarized.

### 8.4 Stokes Vectors and Jones Vectors

#### Jones Vectors

A fully polarized monochromatic beam is represented by a **Jones vector**:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde{E}_x \\ \tilde{E}_y \end{bmatrix}
$$

This is a complex field, carrying phase information.

The inner product of Jones vectors is meaningful: it genuinely measures the angle between two polarization states. Jones vectors can only describe polarized light.

#### Stokes Vectors

A complete description of partially polarized light uses four measurable intensities to define the Stokes vector:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0 &= 2I_0 &&\text{(total irradiance)} \\
S_1 &= 2I_1 - 2I_0 &&\text{(horizontal preference)} \\
S_2 &= 2I_2 - 2I_0 &&\text{(+45° preference)} \\
S_3 &= 2I_3 - 2I_0 &&\text{(right circular preference)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $I_0$ is measured through an isotropic filter, $I_1$ through a $90^{\circ}$ polarizer, $I_2$ through a $+45^{\circ}$ polarizer, and $I_3$ through a right-circular polarizer. Typically each component is normalized by dividing by $S_0$.

If the electric field is written as:

$$
E_x(z, t) = E_{0x}(t)\cos\left(kz-\omega t + \epsilon_x(t)\right), \qquad E_y(z, t) = E_{0y}(t)\cos\left(kz-\omega t + \epsilon_y(t)\right), \qquad \epsilon(t) \equiv \epsilon_y(t) - \epsilon_x(t),
$$

Expressed in terms of time-averaged field components:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0 &= \langle E_{0x}^{2} \rangle_T + \langle E_{0y}^{2} \rangle_T \\
S_1 &= \langle E_{0x}^{2} \rangle_T - \langle E_{0y}^{2} \rangle_T \\
S_2 &= \langle 2E_{0x}E_{0y}\cos\epsilon \rangle_T \\
S_3 &= -\langle 2E_{0x}E_{0y}\sin\epsilon \rangle_T
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\epsilon = \epsilon_y - \epsilon_x$ is the relative phase.

:::warning[Erratum]

Hecht's book (5th Edition) gives: $S_3 = \langle 2E_{0x}E_{0y}\sin\epsilon \rangle_T$, which is problematic. When right-handed circularly polarized light is defined as $(1, -i)$ (Jones vector), the computed Stokes vector is $(1, 0, 0, -1)$, contradicting the table given in his book.

:::

The inner product of Stokes vectors has no meaning. Their sum does represent the superposition of light, but phase information is not preserved. Stokes vectors can describe unpolarized light.

### 8.5 Matrix Representation of Polarization Optical Elements

The effect of optical elements on polarization states can be described by two types of matrices: **Jones matrices** ($2\times 2$ complex matrices, applicable only to fully polarized light) and **Mueller matrices** ($4\times 4$ real matrices, applicable to arbitrary polarization states). Jones calculus: $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_t = \mathbf{J}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_i$; Mueller calculus: $\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{S}_i$. Cascaded elements are multiplied from right to left in the order of the optical path.

**Phase convention**: a quarter-wave plate with its fast axis horizontal carries a global phase $e^{i\pi/4}$, causing the fast- and slow-axis components to acquire symmetric phase shifts of $\pm i\pi/4$.

## Chapter 9: Interference

### 9.1 Conditions for Interference

Two waves produce a stable interference pattern when:
1. They have the same frequency;
2. Coherence:
   - Temporal coherence: ensures the optical path difference is within the coherence length.
   - Spatial coherence: ensures that light from different points of the source can interfere.

### 9.2 Fringes of Equal Inclination (Haidinger Fringes)

<img align="right" src="/img/optics/image-16.png" alt="Equal inclination fringes" width="15%"/>

A parallel plate of refractive index $n_f$ and thickness $d$, with media $n_1, n_2$ on either side.

$AD$ optical path:

$$
AD = AC\sin\theta_i = AC\frac{n_f}{n_1}\sin\theta_t = 2d\tan\theta_t \cdot \frac{n_f}{n_1}\sin\theta_t \Longrightarrow \Lambda_1 = 2n_fd\tan\theta_t\sin\theta_t 
$$

$AB-BC$ optical path:

$$
AB+BC = \frac{2d}{\cos\theta_t} \Longrightarrow \Lambda_f = \frac{2n_fd}{\cos\theta_t}
$$

Optical path difference:

$$
\Lambda = \Lambda_1 - \Lambda_f = \frac{2n_fd}{\cos\theta_t}(1-\sin^2\theta_t) = 2n_fd\cos\theta_t.
$$

Light reflected from the upper and lower surfaces satisfies the coherence condition and can produce interference fringes:

1. $n_1 = n_2 = n$ and $n_f > n$ or $n_f < n$. One reflecting surface undergoes a $\pi$ phase jump (one side changes sign in the Fresnel equations): $\delta = k_0\Lambda \pm \pi$, where we take the negative sign to simplify the form. Hence:
- Maximum $\delta = 2m\pi$: $\;d\cos\theta_t = (2m+1)\dfrac{\lambda_f}{4}$;
- Minimum $\delta = (2m\pm 1)\pi$: $\;d\cos\theta_t = 2m\dfrac{\lambda_f}{4}$.

2. $n_1 > n_f > n_2$ or $n_1 < n_f < n_2$. In this case both reflections are external reflections or both are internal reflections, with no relative $\pi$ phase shift. The maximum and minimum conditions are swapped.

These fringes are localized at infinity (or in the focal plane of a lens). For a given $d$, each $\theta_t$ corresponds to a specific order $m$, producing concentric circular rings (Haidinger fringes).

### 9.3 Fringes of Equal Thickness

<img align="right" src="/img/optics/image-17.png" alt="Equal thickness fringes" width="15%"/>

For a thin wedge of small angle $\alpha$ ($\alpha \ll 1$), near normal incidence ($\theta_t \approx \theta_i \approx 0$):

$$
\Lambda = 2n_f d, \qquad d = x\alpha
$$

When one interface has a $\pi$ phase shift, similar to the equal-inclination case: $k_0\Lambda -\pi = 2m\pi$, solving yields:
$$
d_m = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\lambda_f}{2}, \qquad
x_m = \frac{(m + 1/2)}{2\alpha}\,\lambda_f
$$

The fringes are equally spaced straight lines parallel to the apex of the wedge, with spacing $\Delta x = \lambda_f/(2\alpha)$. When a spherical surface contacts a flat surface, Newton's rings are obtained; the derivation is the same as above, with the substitution $x^2=2Rd$.

### 9.4 Interferometers (Michelson)

The Michelson interferometer splits a light beam, sends the two parts along different paths, and then recombines them. A compensating plate (identical to the beam splitter) ensures that both beams pass through the same thickness of glass, balancing dispersion so that white-light fringes can be observed. The fringe pattern can reveal optical path differences as small as a fraction of a wavelength, making the Michelson interferometer an extremely sensitive measurement tool.

### 9.5 Anti-Reflection Coatings — Transfer Matrix Method

A thin dielectric film between two semi-infinite media can suppress reflection through destructive interference.

<img align="right" src="/img/optics/image-18.png" alt="Anti-reflection coating" width="40%"/>

Let the interface be parallel to the $xy$ plane, the normal be along $z$, and the plane of incidence be the $xz$ plane. As shown, the electric field has only a $y$ component ($s$ polarization):

$$
\vec E = E_y \hat y.
$$

The total electric field inside the layer is written as:

$$
E_y(z)=A e^{ik_z z}+B e^{-ik_z z},
$$

where:

$$
k_z=n_1k_0\cos\theta_t, \qquad k_0=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}.
$$

$\lambda_0$ is the vacuum wavelength. The transverse phase factor $e^{ik_xx}$ has been omitted here because it is conserved along the horizontal direction and merely yields Snell's law. $\theta_t$ here refers to $\theta_{iII}$ — I think there's no need for such complicated notation.

For electromagnetic waves we always have:

$$
\vec{B} = \frac{1}{\omega}\left(\vec{k} \times \vec{E} \right), \qquad \vec H = \frac{1}{\mu_0} \vec B,
$$

Deriving the magnetic field:

$$
\vec H = \frac{1}{\omega\mu_0}\vec k\times\vec E.
$$

According to our convention $\vec k=(k_x,0,k_z)$ and $\vec E=(0,E,0)$, thus

$$
\vec k\times\vec E = (-k_zE,0,k_xE).
$$

Hence

$$
H_x = -\frac{k_z}{\omega\mu_0}E = -\frac{n_1\cos\theta_t}{\mu_0 c}E = - \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0}{\mu_0}}n\cos\theta_t E
$$

The $H_z$ component is neglected — it is uniquely determined directly by the electric field and is not involved in the boundary condition discussion.

Define:

$$
\gamma_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0}{\mu_0}} n_1\cos\theta_t
$$

Thus the total magnetic field can be written as:

$$
H(z) = -\gamma_1 A e^{ik_z z} + \gamma_1 B e^{-ik_z z}.
$$

Note: the film thickness is $d$, so the field at $I$ is:

$$
E(0)=A+B, \qquad H(0)=\gamma_1(B-A).
$$

Field at $II$:

$$
E(d)=Ae^{i\delta}+Be^{-i\delta}, \qquad H(d) = \gamma_1 \left(Be^{-i\delta} - Ae^{i\delta}\right),
$$

where:

$$
\delta = k_z d = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}n_1d\cos\theta_t
$$

Manipulating the field at $II$, we solve inversely:

$$
A = e^{-i\delta} \frac12 \left(E(d)-\frac{H(d)}{\gamma_1}\right),
\qquad
B = e^{i\delta} \frac12 \left(E(d)+\frac{H(d)}{\gamma_1}\right).
$$

Substituting back into the field at $I$:

$$
E(0) = \cos\delta\,E(d) + \frac{i\sin\delta}{\gamma_1} H(d),
$$
$$
H(0)= i\gamma_1\sin\delta\,E(d) + \cos\delta\,H(d),
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
E(0)\\
H(0)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\delta & \dfrac{i\sin\delta}{\gamma_1}
\\[6pt]
i\gamma_1\sin\delta & \cos\delta
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
E(d)\\
H(d)
\end{pmatrix}
$$

This is the characteristic matrix $\mathcal{M}$ of a single-layer film. Switching back to Hecht's notation:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
E_I\\
H_I
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(k_0h) & \dfrac{i\sin(k_0h)}{\gamma_1}
\\[6pt]
i\gamma_1\sin(k_0h) & \cos(k_0h)
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
E_{II}\\
H_{II}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

where $\delta = k_z d = k_0 h$, and $h$ is defined as:

$$
h = \frac{k_zd}{k_0} = n_1 d \cos\theta_{iII}.
$$

For a $p$-layer film, the total matrix is the product $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{M}_1 \mathcal{M}_2 \cdots \mathcal{M}_p$. Note: rewrite the above equation as:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} E_{iI} + E_{rI} \\ -\gamma_0(E_{iI} - E_{rI}) \end{pmatrix}
= \mathcal{M} \begin{pmatrix} E_{tII} \\ -\gamma_s E_{tII} \end{pmatrix}
$$

That is, decompose the electric field into incident and reflected components, and express the magnetic field ($\vec{H}$) in terms of the electric field.

The reflection and transmission coefficients are

$$
r \equiv \frac{E_{rI}}{E_{iI}}= \frac{\gamma_0 m_{11} + \gamma_0\gamma_s m_{12} - m_{21} - \gamma_s m_{22}}
        {\gamma_0 m_{11} + \gamma_0\gamma_s m_{12} + m_{21} + \gamma_s m_{22}},
\qquad
t \equiv \frac{E_{tII}}{E_{iI}} = \frac{2\gamma_0}{\gamma_0 m_{11} + \gamma_0\gamma_s m_{12} + m_{21} + \gamma_s m_{22}}
$$

where:

$$
\gamma_{0} = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0}{\mu_0}} n_1\cos\theta_{iI}, \qquad \gamma_{s} = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0}{\mu_0}} n_s\cos\theta_{tII},
$$

Example: single-layer quarter-wave anti-reflection coating: normal incidence ($\theta_{iI} = \theta_{iII} = \theta_{tII} = 0$), when $k_0 h = \pi/2$ (i.e., thickness $d = \lambda_0/4$):

$$
R = \frac{(n_0 n_s - n_1^{2})^{2}}{(n_0 n_s + n_1^{2})^{2}}
$$

When $n_0n_s = n_1^2$, $R = 0$, and there is no reflection.

Double-layer anti-reflection coating: two such films stacked, with refractive indices $n_1, n_2$, both satisfying $k_0h=\pi/2$, under normal incidence:
$$
\mathcal M = \mathcal M_1 \mathcal M_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i/\gamma_1 \\ i\gamma_1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i/\gamma_2 \\ i\gamma_2 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -n_2/n_1 & 0 \\ 0 & -n_1/n_2 \end{pmatrix},
$$

Thus the reflection coefficient at the second surface:

$$
R_2 = \left(\frac{n_2^2n_0-n_sn_1^2}{n_2^2n_0+n_sn_1^2}\right)^2,
$$

Therefore, when the following condition is satisfied:

$$
\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)^{2} = \frac{n_s}{n_0}
$$

the reflection coefficient is $0$.

## Chapter 10: Diffraction

### 10.1 Ideal Slit (Zero Width)

<img align="right" src="/img/optics/image-20.png" alt="Single slit lemma" width="30%"/>

Consider a slit as shown. By the Huygens-Fresnel principle, every point on it can be regarded as an independent point source emitting a spherical wave:

$$
E = \left(\frac{\bm \varepsilon_0}{r}\right) \sin(\omega t - kr),
$$

where $\bm \varepsilon_0$ is called the source strength. We divide the slit into $N$ equal segments and take a segment of length $dy$:

$$
dE = N\frac{dy}{D} \left(\frac{\bm \varepsilon_0}{r}\right) \sin(\omega t - kr) = \frac{N\bm\varepsilon_0}{D}\frac{\sin(\omega t - kr)}{r} dy,
$$

Clearly, as $N \to \infty$, the source strength of a single source must tend to $0$ so that the slit as a whole emits a finite-strength electromagnetic wave. Hence we define:

$$
\bm \varepsilon_L = \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{N\bm\varepsilon_0}{D},
$$

Then the field at point $P$ can be written as:

$$
dE = \bm \varepsilon_L \frac{\sin(\omega t - kr)}{r} dy \Longrightarrow E = \bm \varepsilon_L\int_{-D/2}^{D/2}\frac{\sin(\omega t - kr)}{r} dy,
$$

where $r = r(y)$ depends on the choice of point $P$. When $R \gg D$, the finite slit approximates a point source, and the amplitude of the wave from each point on the slit can be considered approximately equal:

$$
dE = \bm \varepsilon_L \frac{\sin(\omega t - kr)}{R} dy,
$$

But phase variations are more sensitive and require retaining a higher order; otherwise the slit would be treated entirely as a point source:

$$
r = R-y\sin\theta,
$$

Thus the field:

$$
E = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L}{R}\int_{-D/2}^{D/2}\sin\left[\omega t - k(R-y\sin\theta)\right] dy = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L D}{R} \frac{\sin\left[(kD/2)\sin\theta\right]}{(kD/2)\sin\theta} \sin(\omega t - kR),
$$

Let:

$$
\beta = \frac{kD}{2}\sin\theta
$$

Hence:

$$
E = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L D}{R} \frac{\sin\beta}{\beta} \sin(\omega t - kR),
$$

Or in terms of intensity:

$$
I(\theta) = I(0) \left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2,
$$

where $I(0) = \dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\bm \varepsilon_L D}{R}\right)^2$.

### 10.2 Single Slit

<img align="right" src="/img/optics/image-19.png" alt="Single slit" width="30%"/>

:::note

The width of the single slit in the figure is greatly exaggerated.

:::

A slit of width $b$ (length $\ell \gg \lambda$) can be decomposed along the $z$ direction into infinitely many zero-width slits, which can be approximated as point sources. Thus, it is equivalent to the ideal slit derived earlier:

$$
I(\theta) = I(0)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2},
\qquad
\beta = \frac{kb}{2}\sin\theta,
$$

Its extrema:

$$
\frac{dI}{d\beta} = I(0)\frac{2\sin\beta(\beta\cos\beta-\sin\beta)}{\beta^3} = 0 \Longrightarrow \sin\beta=0 \;\text{or}\; \beta = \tan\beta
$$

- **Principal maximum** ($\theta=0$): $\beta = 0$
- **Minima** ($\sin\beta = 0$): $\beta = \pm\pi, \pm 2\pi, \pm 3\pi, \dots$ or $b\sin\theta_m = m\lambda,\; m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$
- **Secondary maxima** ($\tan\beta = \beta$): $\beta \approx \pm 1.43\pi,\; \pm 2.46\pi,\; \pm 3.47\pi, \dots$

The central maximum contains about 84% of the total diffracted energy.

### 10.3 Multiple Slits

Consider $N$ identical, equally spaced slits. Each slit has width $b$, and the center-to-center spacing between adjacent slits is $a$ ($a > b$). From the [single slit](#102-single-slit) result, the field produced at a far-field point $P$ by a single slit (centered at the origin) is:

$$
E_{\text{single}}(\theta) = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L b}{R}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\,\sin(\omega t - kR),
\qquad
\beta = \frac{kb}{2}\sin\theta.
$$

Now arrange the $N$ slits along the $y$ direction, with the center of the $j$-th slit at $y_j = j a$ ($j = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$). Its distance to point $P$ is:

$$
r_j \approx R - y_j\sin\theta = R - j a\sin\theta,
$$

Under far-field conditions, the amplitude attenuation is approximately the same (denominator all taken as $R$), but the phase differences cannot be neglected. Thus the contribution of the $j$-th slit to the field is:

$$
E_j(\theta) = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L b}{R}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\,\sin\!\big(\omega t - kR + j\cdot ka\sin\theta\big).
$$

Define the phase difference between adjacent slits:

$$
2\alpha \equiv ka\sin\theta,
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\alpha = \frac{ka}{2}\sin\theta.
$$

Then the total field is the coherent superposition of $N$ slits:

$$
E(\theta) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} E_j
          = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L b}{R}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\,
            \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \sin(\omega t - kR + 2j\alpha).
$$

Using Euler's formula, the summation becomes a geometric series:

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{i(\omega t - kR + 2j\alpha)}
= e^{i(\omega t - kR)} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \left(e^{i2\alpha}\right)^{j}
= e^{i(\omega t - kR)}\,\frac{1 - e^{i2N\alpha}}{1 - e^{i2\alpha}}.
$$

Extracting the phase factor:

$$
\frac{1 - e^{i2N\alpha}}{1 - e^{i2\alpha}}
= \frac{e^{iN\alpha}(e^{-iN\alpha} - e^{iN\alpha})}
       {e^{i\alpha}(e^{-i\alpha} - e^{i\alpha})}
= e^{i(N-1)\alpha}\,\frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}.
$$

Taking the imaginary part gives the real sine sum:

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \sin(\omega t - kR + 2j\alpha)
= \frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\,\sin\!\big(\omega t - kR + (N-1)\alpha\big).
$$

Thus the total field is:

$$
E(\theta) = \frac{\bm \varepsilon_L b}{R}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\,
                 \frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\,
                 \sin\!\big(\omega t - kR + (N-1)\alpha\big).
$$

The irradiance is proportional to the square of the amplitude; time-averaging eliminates the $\sin^{2}$ term, yielding:

$$
I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}
                 \left(\frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)^{2}.
$$

where $I_0$ is the intensity produced by a single slit in the $\theta=0$ direction, and $I(0)=N^2I_0$.

#### Extrema

**Principal maxima:** when $\alpha = m\pi$ ($m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$), both numerator and denominator are zero; using L'Hôpital's rule:

$$
\lim_{\alpha \to m\pi} \frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}
= \lim_{\alpha \to m\pi} \frac{N\cos N\alpha}{\cos\alpha}
= \pm N,
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\left(\frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)^{2} \to N^{2}.
$$

Thus the angular positions of the principal maxima satisfy:

$$
\alpha = m\pi \;\Longrightarrow\; a\sin\theta_m = m\lambda,
\qquad m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots
$$

This is precisely the grating equation. $m=0$ is the zeroth-order principal maximum (all wavelets in phase), with intensity $N^{2}I_0$.

**Minima (zeros):** numerator is zero while denominator is nonzero:

$$
\sin N\alpha = 0,\; \sin\alpha \neq 0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\alpha = \pm\frac{\pi}{N},\; \pm\frac{2\pi}{N},\; \dots,\; \pm\frac{(N-1)\pi}{N},\;
\pm\frac{(N+1)\pi}{N},\; \dots
$$

That is, there are $N-1$ dark fringes between adjacent principal maxima.

**Secondary maxima:** between two adjacent minima ($\sin N\alpha = 0$) there must be a secondary maximum. The exact position of the secondary maximum is given by setting the derivative of the intensity with respect to $\alpha$ to zero, i.e., the stationary condition of the interference factor $(\sin N\alpha/\sin\alpha)^{2}$:

$$
\frac{d}{d\alpha}\left(\frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right) = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\tan N\alpha = N\tan\alpha. 
$$

This is a transcendental equation. Approximate solution: considering that the numerator varies slowly while the denominator varies rapidly, the maximum should occur near where the numerator attains its maximum:

$$
|\sin N\alpha| \approx 1
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\alpha_p \approx \frac{(2p+1)\pi}{2N}, \qquad p = 1, 2, \dots, N-2.
$$

The intensity of secondary maxima rapidly decays as $N$ increases. There are $N-2$ secondary maxima between adjacent principal maxima.

**Sharpness of principal maxima:** the first zeros on either side of a principal maximum are at $\alpha = m\pi \pm \pi/N$, with angular width $\Delta\alpha = 2\pi/N \propto 1/N$. Thus the more slits, the sharper the principal maxima.

#### Missing Orders

When a minimum of the single-slit diffraction falls exactly on an interference principal maximum, that order is suppressed — this is called a **missing order**. The condition for a single-slit diffraction minimum is:

$$
b\sin\theta = p\lambda,\quad p = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\beta = p\pi.
$$

The condition for an interference principal maximum is:

$$
a\sin\theta = m\lambda,\quad m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots
$$

Combining the two equations:

$$
\frac{a}{b} = \frac{m}{p}.
$$

If $a/b$ is a rational number, then certain interference principal maxima coincide with diffraction minima. The most typical case is $a = m b$ ($m$ integer), where the $\pm m, \pm 2m, \dots$ orders of interference principal maxima disappear. Within the central diffraction peak ($|\beta| < \pi$, i.e., $|\sin\theta| < \lambda/b$) there are $2m-1$ bright interference fringes (namely orders $m = 0, \pm 1, \dots, \pm(m-1)$).

### 10.4 Double Slit

As a special case of $N=2$, the interference factor simplifies to:

$$
\left(\frac{\sin 2\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)^{2}
= \left(\frac{2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)^{2}
= 4\cos^{2}\alpha.
$$

Thus the double-slit intensity formula is:

$$
I_2(\theta) = 4I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha,
\qquad
\alpha = \frac{ka}{2}\sin\theta = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta.
$$

### 10.5 Fourier Connection

The Fraunhofer diffraction pattern is the **Fourier transform** of the aperture function. For example, a periodic rectangular grating (square wave) can be expanded into a Fourier series:

$$
f(x) = \frac{2}{a} + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{4}{a}\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{2m\pi}{a}\right)\cos(m k x)
$$

Each Fourier component corresponds to a specific diffraction order. The sinc envelope reflects the single-slit shape factor, while the discrete orders originate from periodicity. This is the foundation of Fourier optics.

### 10.6 Circular Aperture Diffraction — Airy Disk

![Circular aperture diffraction](/img/optics/image-21.png)

For a circular aperture of radius $a$ (diameter $D = 2a$):

$$
I(\theta) = I(0)\left[\frac{2J_1(ka\sin\theta)}{ka\sin\theta}\right]^{2}
$$

where $J_1$ is the first-order Bessel function. The central maximum is the Airy disk. The first dark ring occurs at

$$
ka\sin\theta = 3.83 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sin\theta_1 \approx 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}
$$

In the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$, the radius of the Airy disk to the first minimum is

$$
q_1 = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda f}{D}
$$

#### Rayleigh Criterion

When the central maximum of one Airy disk from two incoherent point sources falls exactly on the first minimum of the other, the two are just barely resolvable:

$$
(\Delta\theta)_{\min} = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}
$$

### 10.7 Grating Equation

The derivation of the multi-slit principal maximum condition in [§10.3](#103-multiple-slits) assumed light is **normally incident** on the grating. If light is incident at an angle $\theta_i$ (relative to the grating normal), the phase difference between adjacent slits needs to be modified.

Recalling the multi-slit intensity formula:

$$
I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\sin N\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)^{2},
\qquad
\alpha = \frac{ka}{2}\sin\theta,
$$

where $\alpha$ comes from the **path difference** $a\sin\theta$ between adjacent slits to the far-field point $P$. Under oblique incidence, the incident wave itself already has a path difference upon reaching the slits: the $j$-th slit's incident light travels $a\sin\theta_i$ **less** than the $(j-1)$-th slit (taking $\theta_i$ and $\theta$ positive when on the same side of the normal). Thus the **total path difference** between adjacent slits, from source to observation point, becomes:

$$
\Delta r = a\sin\theta - a\sin\theta_i = a(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_i).
$$

Correspondingly, the phase difference between adjacent slits is modified to:

$$
2\alpha' = k\,\Delta r = ka(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_i),
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\alpha' = \frac{ka}{2}(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_i).
$$

The form of the multi-slit intensity formula is **completely unchanged** — simply replace $\alpha$ with $\alpha'$. The principal maximum condition $\alpha' = m\pi$ gives:

$$
a(\sin\theta_m - \sin\theta_i) = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots
$$

This is the **grating equation**. Here $a$ is the grating period (reciprocal of the ruling density); $m$ is the diffraction order. $m=0$ corresponds to the zeroth order — where $\theta_0 = \theta_i$, i.e., the exit direction equals the incident direction (specular reflection/direct transmission), carrying no wavelength information; orders with $|m| \ge 1$ disperse different wavelengths to different angles, which is the basis of the grating as a dispersive element.

### 10.8 Blazed Gratings

The groove faces of a blazed reflection grating are tilted by the **blaze angle** $\gamma$. The single-face diffraction envelope ($\sin\beta/\beta$) is shifted so that its peak coincides with the desired non-zero diffraction order, concentrating most of the diffracted energy into that order for a specific wavelength range.

### 10.9 Angular Dispersion

Angular dispersion measures the **angular separation** between light of different wavelengths produced by a grating. Differentiating both sides of the grating equation (holding the incident angle $\theta_i$ fixed):

$$
a\cos\theta_m \, d\theta = m \, d\lambda
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{a\cos\theta_m}.
$$

Higher orders (larger $|m|$) and finer ruling spacing (smaller $a$) give larger angular dispersion. But angular dispersion is only **one aspect** of spectral resolution — whether spectral lines can be resolved also depends on the intrinsic **width** of each line.

### 10.10 Resolving Power

#### Angular Width of Principal Maxima

Referring back to the conclusion about the sharpness of principal maxima in [§10.3](#103-multiple-slits): the $m$-th order principal maximum is at $\alpha = m\pi$, with first zeros on either side at $\alpha = m\pi \pm \pi/N$. Thus in $\alpha$-space, the **half-angular width** of a principal maximum is $\Delta\alpha = \pi/N$.

Differentiating $\alpha = \frac{ka}{2}\sin\theta = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta$ with respect to $\theta$:

$$
d\alpha = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\cos\theta \, d\theta.
$$

Setting $d\alpha = \Delta\alpha = \pi/N$, we obtain the **half-angular width** of the $m$-th order principal maximum in $\theta$-space (angular distance from peak to first zero):

$$
\boxed{(\Delta\theta)_{\text{half}} = \frac{\lambda}{N a \cos\theta_m}}.
$$

The **full angular width** of a principal maximum (between first zeros on both sides) is $2(\Delta\theta)_{\text{half}} = 2\lambda/(N a\cos\theta_m)$. The larger the number of slits $N$, the narrower the principal maxima — this is the quantitative statement of "sharpness $\propto 1/N$" from §10.3.

#### Rayleigh Criterion → Resolving Power

Now consider two wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ ($\Delta\lambda \ll \lambda$), which produce principal maxima at order $m$ after passing through the grating. The **angular separation** between the two principal maxima is given by the angular dispersion:

$$
\Delta\theta_{\lambda} = \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda}\,\Delta\lambda = \frac{m}{a\cos\theta_m}\,\Delta\lambda.
$$

According to the Rayleigh criterion, when the principal maximum of one spectral line falls exactly on the first zero of the other, the two are **just barely resolvable**. That is, the angular separation must equal the half-angular width:

$$
\Delta\theta_{\lambda} = (\Delta\theta)_{\text{half}}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{m}{a\cos\theta_m}\,\Delta\lambda_{\min} = \frac{\lambda}{N a \cos\theta_m}.
$$

Canceling $a\cos\theta_m$, we obtain the minimum resolvable wavelength difference:

$$
\Delta\lambda_{\min} = \frac{\lambda}{mN}.
$$

The **resolving power** is defined as:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{R} \equiv \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda_{\min}} = mN}.
$$

Using the grating equation $a(\sin\theta_m - \sin\theta_i) = m\lambda$, this can also be written as:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{R} = \frac{N a (\sin\theta_m - \sin\theta_i)}{\lambda}}.
$$

Two key factors are clear at a glance: **$m$** — using higher orders improves resolving power; **$N$** — the more total rulings (larger grating or denser rulings), the sharper the spectral lines and the higher the resolving power.
