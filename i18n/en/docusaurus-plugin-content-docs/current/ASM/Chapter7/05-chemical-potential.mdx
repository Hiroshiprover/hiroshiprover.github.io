# 7.5 Chemical Potential

## Thermodynamics.

In the canonical ensemble, the Helmholtz free energy $A$ is a function of $N$, $V$, and $T$, with $N$ fixed. In the grand canonical ensemble, the particle number is allowed to vary, so the chemical potential $\mu$ enters through an additional term $\mu\,dN$. Taking these dependencies into account, we obtain:

<Anchor id="eq-7-47" />
$$
dA = -PdV - SdT + \mu dN. \tag{7.47}
$$

By applying the Legendre transformation $U = A + TS$, we obtain the first law of thermodynamics in its general form:

<Anchor id="eq-7-48" />
$$
dU = TdS - PdV + \mu dN. \tag{7.48}
$$

The Gibbs free energy is defined by another Legendre transformation: $G = A + PV$. Its differential form is:

<Anchor id="eq-7-49" />
$$
dG = VdP - SdT + \mu dN. \tag{7.49}
$$

From Eqs. [(7.47)](#eq-7-47)-[(7.49)](#eq-7-49), we obtain the following expressions for the chemical potential $\mu$ under different conditions:

<Anchor id="eq-7-50" />
$$
\mu = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{V,S} = \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial N}\right)_{V,T} = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N}\right)_{P,T}. \tag{7.50}
$$

As an example, let us derive the chemical potential of an ideal gas, for which the canonical partition function for a system of $N$ particles is given by:

<Anchor id="eq-7-51" />
$$
Q_N(V,T) = \frac{1}{N!h^{3N}}\int d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p\,e^{-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{3N}p_i^2/(2m)} = \frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{V}{\lambda^3}\right)^N, \tag{7.51}
$$

<Anchor id="eq-7-52" />
$$
\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mkT}},\quad (\text{thermal wavelength}). \tag{7.52}
$$

The grand partition function is then:

<Anchor id="eq-7-53" />
$$
\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} z^N Q_N(V,T) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{zV}{\lambda^3}\right)^N = e^{zV/\lambda^3}. \tag{7.53}
$$

The average particle number can be derived from Eq. [(7.36)](./03-grand-canonical-ensemble.mdx#eq-7-36)

<Anchor id="eq-7-54" />
$$
\langle N\rangle = z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = \frac{zV}{\lambda^3}. \tag{7.54}
$$

Finally, we obtain the chemical potential of an ideal gas in the following form

<Anchor id="eq-7-55" />
$$
\mu = kT\ln(\lambda^3 n),\quad n = \langle N\rangle/V. \tag{7.55}
$$

Furthermore, the well-known equation of state for an ideal gas can also be derived: $PV/(kT) = \ln\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = zV/\lambda^3 = \langle N\rangle$.

Recall that when the isothermal compressibility of the gas is finite, the grand partition function $\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T)$ can be well approximated by its maximum term $W(\bar{N})$. We shall demonstrate this statement using the ideal gas model. Note that the condition for maximizing $\ln W(N)$ is given by:

$$
\delta\ln W(N) = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \ln W(N+1) - \ln W(N) = \ln\left[\frac{z^{N+1}Q_{N+1}(V,T)}{z^N Q_N(V,T)}\right] = 0.
$$

Substituting Eq. [(7.51)](#eq-7-51) into this equation, we obtain

$$
\bar{N} + 1 = \frac{zV}{\lambda^3}.
$$

Hence, $\bar{N} \approx \langle N\rangle$ in the thermodynamic limit. Then,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln W(\bar{N}) &= \ln\left[\frac{1}{\bar{N}!}\left(\frac{zV}{\lambda^3}\right)^{\bar{N}}\right] = \ln\left[\frac{(\bar{N}+1)^{\bar{N}}}{\bar{N}!}\right]\\
&\approx \bar{N}\ln(\bar{N}+1) - \bar{N}\ln\bar{N} + \bar{N} = \bar{N}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\bar{N}}\right) + \bar{N}\\
&= 1 + \bar{N} = \frac{zV}{\lambda^3} = \ln\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T).
\end{aligned}
$$

This equation justifies the conclusion that the grand partition function can be approximated by the most probable value of $W(N)$

$$
\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} W(N) \approx W(\bar{N}).
$$

## Conservation of Particle Number

In this part, we explore an interesting application of statistical physics to particle physics.

As established earlier, in the thermodynamic limit the grand canonical ensemble becomes equivalent to the canonical ensemble, since relative particle-number fluctuations vanish for large systems. In practice, the grand canonical ensemble provides a convenient approximation to the canonical ensemble even when the particle number is strictly conserved; the chemical potential then acts as a Lagrange multiplier that enforces a fixed average particle number.

In particle physics, baryon number conservation is a fundamental law: the total number of baryons (e.g., protons, neutrons) minus antibaryons is conserved. For instance, a proton can be created or annihilated alongside an antiproton. Thus, the proton number itself is not conserved. However, at low temperatures, thermal energy is insufficient to produce baryon-antibaryon pairs, and antibaryons are absent. In this regime, the proton (and neutron) number is effectively conserved.

A similar situation arises for electrons. At low temperatures, electron-positron pair production is negligible, and positrons are essentially absent, so the electron number is effectively conserved. At high temperatures, where the thermal energy exceeds the electron rest energy $mc^2$, the conserved quantity is instead the difference between the electron and positron numbers.

We now apply the grand canonical ensemble to analyze equilibrium in systems that allow particle–antiparticle pair creation and annihilation. The effective Hamiltonian is:

<Anchor id="eq-7-56" />
$$
\mathscr{H} = \mathscr{H}_1 + \mathscr{H}_2 - \mu(N_1 - N_2), \tag{7.56}
$$

where subscripts “1” and “2” denote particles and antiparticles, respectively, and $\mu$ is introduced as a Lagrange multiplier so that $N_1$ and $N_2$ can be treated as unconstrained variables. The grand partition function is:

<Anchor id="eq-7-57" />
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{Q} &= \sum_{N_1=0}^{\infty}\sum_{N_2=0}^{\infty} e^{\beta\mu(N_1-N_2)} Q_{N_1} Q_{N_2}\\
&= \sum_{N_1=0}^{\infty}\sum_{N_2=0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta[A(N_1)+A(N_2)-\mu(N_1-N_2)]}\\
&\approx e^{-\beta[A(\bar{N}_1)+A(\bar{N}_2)-\mu(\bar{N}_1-\bar{N}_2)]},
\end{aligned} \tag{7.57}
$$

where only the dominant term is retained. The values $\bar{N}_1$ and $\bar{N}_2$ are determined by minimizing the exponent:

<Anchor id="eq-7-58" />
$$
\frac{\partial A(N_1)}{\partial N_1} = \mu,\quad \frac{\partial A(N_2)}{\partial N_2} = -\mu. \tag{7.58}
$$

To evaluate the Helmholtz free energies $A(N_1)$ and $A(N_2)$, a relativistic treatment would be preferable. For simplicity, however, we adopt the non-relativistic limit, in which the energy, including the rest energy, is given by $E = mc^2 + \frac{p^2}{2m}$. Using Eq. [(7.55)](#eq-7-55) with the chemical potential shifted by the rest energy, we obtain:

<Anchor id="eq-7-59" />
$$
kT\ln(\lambda^3 n_1) + mc^2 = \mu,\quad kT\ln(\lambda^3 n_2) + mc^2 = -\mu, \tag{7.59}
$$

where $n_1$ and $n_2$ are the most probable densities of particles and antiparticles, respectively. Adding these equations yields:

<Anchor id="eq-7-60" />
$$
\lambda^6 n_1 n_2 = e^{-2mc^2/(kT)}. \tag{7.60}
$$

The electron rest energy corresponds to a temperature of about $6\times10^9$ K, whereas the proton rest energy corresponds to a temperature roughly 2000 times higher. Hence, at ordinary temperatures the right-hand side of Eq. [(7.60)](#eq-7-60) is negligible, implying that at least one of $n_1$ and $n_2$ must be vanishingly small. This explains why antiparticles can be safely neglected when $kT \ll mc^2$.

## Chemical Equilibrium

In this part, we show application of statistical physics in chemistry.

Consider a very familiar chemical reaction

$$
2\mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2 = 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}.
$$

It can also be rewritten as

$$
2\mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2 - 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} = 0.
$$

Accordingly, a general reaction can be expressed as

<Anchor id="eq-7-61" />
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\nu_i X_i = 0, \tag{7.61}
$$

where $\nu_i > 0$ for reactants and $\nu_i < 0$ for products.

The conservation law for this reaction is

<Anchor id="eq-7-62" />
$$
\frac{\delta N_1}{\nu_1} = \frac{\delta N_2}{\nu_2}\cdots = \frac{\delta N_K}{\nu_K}, \tag{7.62}
$$

where $\delta N_i$ is the number variation of the particle of the $i$th type. It then allows us to write

<Anchor id="eq-7-63" />
$$
\delta N_i = \nu_i\delta N,\quad i = 1,\cdots,K. \tag{7.63}
$$

When the reaction balance is reached at constant $V$ and $T$, the total Helmholtz free energy should be minimized,

<Anchor id="eq-7-64" />
$$
0 = \delta A = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\delta A_i = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial N_i}\delta N_i = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_i\nu_i\delta N. \tag{7.64}
$$

Since $\delta N \neq 0$, the condition for reaction equilibrium is

<Anchor id="eq-7-65" />
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_i\nu_i = 0, \tag{7.65}
$$

where $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential of the molecule $K_i$. If the reaction proceeds at constant $P$ and $T$, we may equivalently use the Gibbs free energy, and the same condition is obtained.
