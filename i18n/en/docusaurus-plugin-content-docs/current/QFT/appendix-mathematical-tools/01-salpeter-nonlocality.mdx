# A. Nonlocality of the Salpeter Equation and the Probability Current

Let us discuss in detail the nonlocality mentioned in the main text.

## Locality

Let us first define locality rigorously:

:::tip[Locality]
A translation-invariant linear operator acts on sufficiently well-behaved test functions as
$$
(\hat Df)(\vec x)=\int d^3y\,K(\vec x-\vec y)f(\vec y),
$$
where $K$ is called the integral kernel; it may be an ordinary function, or a distribution such as the Dirac delta function and its derivatives. If $K$ is supported only at the origin (roughly speaking, it is nonzero only at the origin), the operator is called **local**.
:::

A few examples:
1. The kernel of the identity operator is $\delta^{(3)}(\vec x-\vec y)$, which is obviously local.
2. The kernel of a derivative operator is $\partial_{i}\delta^{(3)}(\vec x-\vec y)$, which is also local. Hence any linear combination of derivative operators of finite order is local as well.
3. For a translation, $f(\vec x)\mapsto f(\vec x+\vec a)$, the kernel is $\delta^{(3)}(\vec x-\vec y+\vec a)$; when $\vec a\ne0$ it reads the value of the function at another point, so the operator is nonlocal.

## Fourier transform
:::info[Fourier transform convention]
We use the following Fourier transform convention:
$$
\widetilde f(\vec k)=\int d^3x\,f(\vec x)e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec x},
\qquad
f(\vec x)=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\widetilde f(\vec k)e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}.
$$
:::

Next we prove a useful lemma. Mathematicians tell us that a distribution supported only at the origin can be written as a linear combination of finitely many derivatives of the delta function,
$$
f(\vec x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a^{j_1\cdots j_n}
\partial_{j_1}\cdots\partial_{j_n}\delta^{(3)}(\vec x),
$$
where each $j_r$ takes the values $1,2,3$ and repeated indices are summed; the $n=0$ term is the delta function itself. If you do not know the Einstein summation convention, you may first consult [this section](../02-lorentz-group.mdx).

Its Fourier transform is therefore
$$
\widetilde{f}(\vec k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \int d^3x\, a^{j_1\cdots j_n} \partial_{j_1}\cdots\partial_{j_n}\delta^{(3)}(\vec x) e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec x},
$$
and by the definition of the derivative of a delta function (which extracts the corresponding derivative of the function at that point) we have
$$
\widetilde{f}(\vec k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} i^n a^{j_1\cdots j_n} k_{j_1}\cdots k_{j_n},
$$
so $\widetilde f(\vec k)$ is merely a polynomial in $k$ of finite degree.

:::note
Strictly speaking, the derivative of the delta function is defined by integration by parts.
:::

Conversely, for any power of $k$ (in one dimension) we can compute the inverse transform:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\, k^m e^{ik\cdot x} 
&= (-i)^m \int \frac{dk}{2\pi}\, (ik)^m e^{ik\cdot x}\\
&= (-i)^m \int \frac{dk}{2\pi}\, \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^m e^{ik\cdot x} \\
&= (-i)^m  \left(\frac{d}{d x}\right)^m \int \frac{dk}{2\pi}\, e^{ik\cdot x} \\
&= (-i)^m \delta^{(m)}(x),
\end{aligned} 
$$
so the inverse transform of any polynomial in $k$ of finite degree contains only the delta function and finitely many of its derivatives. The generalization to three dimensions is natural (but tedious), and we will not carry out the calculation in detail here.

We therefore have the lemma:
$$
\boxed{
f(\vec x) \text{ is supported only at the origin}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\widetilde f(\vec k)\text{ is a polynomial of finite degree}.
}
$$

Note that the degree must be finite. A counterexample is easy to give: the inverse transform of $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta^{(n)}(x)/n!$ gives $e^{ik}$, which is not a polynomial.

:::note
Infinite series of distributions are a delicate matter; here we have only written one down formally. In fact such a series usually does not converge, and even when it does it usually does not converge to an element of the space we are considering. We will not go into this here.
:::

Let us return to the definition of locality given earlier. Using the Fourier transform we can write it in a nicer form:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\hat Df)(\vec x)&=\int d^3y\,K(\vec x-\vec y)f(\vec y)\\
&=\int d^3y\,K(\vec x-\vec y)\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\widetilde f(\vec k)e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec y}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\int d^3y\,K(\vec x-\vec y)\widetilde f(\vec k)e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec y}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\, \widetilde f(\vec k) e^{i\vec k \cdot \vec x}\int d^3y\,K(\vec x-\vec y)e^{-i\vec k\cdot\left(\vec x - \vec y\right)}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\, \widetilde{K}(\vec k) \widetilde f(\vec k) e^{i\vec k \cdot \vec x}.
\end{aligned} 
$$
Note that we have quietly used $\int d^3y = \int d^3(y-x) = \int d^3(x-y)$, with the integral taken over all space. This is because $x$ is finite, so the infinite limits of integration are unchanged, and the minus sign produced by exchanging $x$ and $y$ is absorbed by exchanging the limits. We will use this trick often, so it is worth remembering.

## Nonlocality of the square-root operator

The Salpeter equation reads
$$
i\partial_t\psi=\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi,
$$

In the main text we only wrote down this equation formally and did not give it a rigorous definition. We must first define how the square-root operator acts on functions before we can discuss it further. Recall from finite-dimensional linear algebra that a Hermitian operator has a spectral decomposition:
$$
\hat{A}=\sum_n a_n |n\rangle\langle n|,
$$
If $\hat{A}$ is a positive operator, that is, every eigenvalue $a_n$ is non-negative, then there is a unique positive square-root operator:
$$
\sqrt{\hat{A}} \equiv \sum_n \sqrt{a_n} |n\rangle\langle n|.
$$

In infinite dimensions the question is much more delicate. But we only consider wave functions that are smooth and decay sufficiently fast at infinity, in which case $-\nabla^2+m^2$ is indeed a positive operator. Given the baby nature of these notes, we will not reproduce the proof and simply use this conclusion. First note that
$$
\left(-\nabla^2 +m^2\right)e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}= (k^{2}+m^2) e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}
$$
so $-\nabla^2 +m^2$ has eigenvalues $k^{2}+m^2$ with eigenvectors $e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}$. We therefore define the action of the square-root operator by
$$
\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}
\equiv\sqrt{k^2+m^2}\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}.
$$
Now expanding $\psi$ in a Fourier integral, we obtain
$$
\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi(\vec x,t)
=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,
\sqrt{k^2+m^2}\,\widetilde\psi(\vec k,t)e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}.
$$
Comparing with the expression for locality obtained just now, we have
$$
\widetilde K(\vec k)=\sqrt{k^2+m^2}.
$$
By the lemma proved above, $\sqrt{k^2+m^2}$ is not a polynomial in $k$ of finite degree, so $K$ cannot be supported only at the origin; hence the square-root operator is nonlocal.

## Conservation of total probability

The following discussion rests on interpreting $\psi$ as the spatial probability distribution of a single particle. We know that in non-relativistic quantum mechanics
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int d^3x\,|\psi|^2 = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial t} |\psi|^2 + \nabla \cdot \vec j = 0, \qquad \vec{j} = \frac{i}{2m}\left(\psi^* \nabla \psi - \psi \nabla \psi^*\right).
$$

So for the Salpeter equation we try to find the same result. From the equation of motion and its complex conjugate,
$$
\partial_t\psi=-i\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi,
\qquad
\partial_t\psi^*=i\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi^*,
$$
we obtain
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int d^3x\,|\psi|^2
=i\int d^3x\,\left[
\bigl(\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi^*\bigr)\psi
-\psi^*\bigl(\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi\bigr)
\right].
$$

Let us compute the integral in the second term:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&\equiv\int d^3x\,\psi^*\bigl(\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi\bigr)\\
&=\int d^3x\,\int \frac{d^3 q}{(2\pi)^3} \widetilde{\psi}^*\bigl(\vec q , t\bigr) e^{-i\vec q \cdot \vec x} \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,
\sqrt{k^2+m^2}\,\widetilde\psi\bigl(\vec k , t\bigr) e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3k\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\sqrt{k^2+m^2}\,\widetilde\psi^*\bigl(\vec q , t\bigr) \widetilde\psi\bigl(\vec k , t\bigr)
\int d^3x\,e^{i(\vec k-\vec q)\cdot\vec x}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3k\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\sqrt{k^2+m^2}\,\widetilde\psi^* \widetilde\psi \cdot 
(2\pi)^3 \delta(\vec k - \vec q) \\
&=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,
\sqrt{k^2+m^2}\,|\widetilde\psi(\vec k,t)|^2\in\mathbb R.
\end{aligned}
$$
Note that in the Fourier transform of $\psi^*$ the factor $e^{i \vec q \cdot \vec x}$ must also change sign.

The integral in the first term is $I^*$, and $I$ is real, so
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int d^3x\,|\psi|^2=i(I^*-I)=0.
$$
For the Salpeter equation, then, the total probability is conserved. We may therefore try to find a probability current.

## The bilinear probability current and its locality

Both $|\psi|^2$ and its time derivative are bilinear in $\psi^*$ and $\psi$, so if the continuity equation holds, then $\vec{j}$ must also be bilinear in $\psi^*$ and $\psi$. We therefore write the current as
$$
\vec j(t,\vec x)=\int d^3y\,d^3z\,
\vec K(\vec x-\vec y,\vec x-\vec z)\psi^*(t,\vec y)\psi(t,\vec z).
$$
Here $\vec K$ is vector-valued. If $\vec K(\vec x,\vec y)$ is supported only at $\vec x=\vec y=\vec0$, computing the current requires only the wave function and finitely many of its derivatives at the same point, and we call such a current local.

The Fourier transform of a function of two variables is defined as
$$
\widetilde{\vec K}(\vec p,\vec q)
=\int d^3x\,d^3y\,\vec K(\vec x,\vec y)
e^{-i\vec p\cdot\vec x}e^{-i\vec q\cdot\vec y}.
$$
As in the single-variable case, a kernel supported only at the origin can be written as
$$
\vec K(\vec x,\vec y)
=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{n=0}^{N}
\vec a^{j_1\cdots j_m;k_1\cdots k_n}
\bigl[\partial_{x^{j_1}}\cdots\partial_{x^{j_m}}\delta^{(3)}(\vec x)\bigr]
\bigl[\partial_{y^{k_1}}\cdots\partial_{y^{k_n}}\delta^{(3)}(\vec y)\bigr].
$$
Integrating by parts in each variable, every derivative brings a factor $ip_i$ or $iq_j$, so
$$
\widetilde{\vec K}(\vec p,\vec q)
=\sum_{m,n}\vec a^{j_1\cdots j_m;k_1\cdots k_n}
(ip_{j_1})\cdots(ip_{j_m})(iq_{k_1})\cdots(iq_{k_n})
$$
is a vector-valued polynomial of finite degree. Conversely, the inverse transform of such a polynomial contains only the delta function and finitely many of its derivatives. Hence, if the probability current is local, $\widetilde{\vec K}(\vec p,\vec q)$ must be a polynomial of finite degree.

## Solving the continuity equation

According to our earlier computation,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t|\psi|^2 &= i\left[
\bigl(\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi^*\bigr)\psi
-\psi^*\bigl(\sqrt{-\nabla^2+m^2}\,\psi\bigr)
\right]\\
&=i\int\frac{d^3p\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\left(\sqrt{p^2+m^2}-\sqrt{q^2+m^2}\right)
\widetilde\psi^*(\vec p,t)\widetilde\psi(\vec q,t)
e^{i(\vec q-\vec p)\cdot\vec x} 
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the inverse Fourier transform of the kernel of the current and carrying out the $\vec y,\vec z$ integrals, we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec j(t,\vec x)
&=\int\frac{d^3p\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\widetilde{\vec K}(\vec p,\vec q)e^{i(\vec p+\vec q)\cdot\vec x}
\left[\int d^3y\,e^{-i\vec p\cdot\vec y}\psi^*(t,\vec y)\right]
\left[\int d^3z\,e^{-i\vec q\cdot\vec z}\psi(t,\vec z)\right]\\
&=\int\frac{d^3p\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\widetilde{\vec K}(\vec p,\vec q)\widetilde\psi^*(-\vec p,t)
\widetilde\psi(\vec q,t)e^{i(\vec p+\vec q)\cdot\vec x}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3p\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\widetilde{\vec K}(-\vec p,\vec q)\widetilde\psi^*(\vec p,t)
\widetilde\psi(\vec q,t)e^{i(\vec q-\vec p)\cdot\vec x}.
\end{aligned}
$$
where in the last line we changed the variable $\vec p$ to $-\vec p$. Taking the divergence then gives
$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{j}=i\int\frac{d^3p\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\widetilde{\vec K}(-\vec p,\vec q)\cdot(\vec q-\vec p)\,\widetilde\psi^*(\vec p,t)
\widetilde\psi(\vec q,t)\,e^{i(\vec q-\vec p)\cdot\vec x}.
$$
The continuity equation reads
$$
\partial_t |\psi|^2 = - \nabla \cdot \vec{j}
$$
and comparing the two sides gives
$$
\boxed{
(\vec p-\vec q)\cdot\widetilde{\vec K}(-\vec p,\vec q)
=\sqrt{p^2+m^2}-\sqrt{q^2+m^2}.
}
$$
Using the difference of squares,
$$
\sqrt{p^2+m^2}-\sqrt{q^2+m^2}
=\frac{(\vec p-\vec q)\cdot(\vec p+\vec q)}
{\sqrt{p^2+m^2}+\sqrt{q^2+m^2}},
$$
so the most convenient choice is
$$
\widetilde{\vec K}(-\vec p,\vec q)
=\frac{\vec p+\vec q}{\sqrt{p^2+m^2}+\sqrt{q^2+m^2}},
$$
that is,
$$
\widetilde{\vec K}(\vec p,\vec q)
=\frac{\vec q-\vec p}{\sqrt{p^2+m^2}+\sqrt{q^2+m^2}}.
$$
The corresponding probability current is
$$
\vec j(t,\vec x)=\int\frac{d^3p\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^6}\,
\frac{\vec p+\vec q}{\sqrt{p^2+m^2}+\sqrt{q^2+m^2}}
\widetilde\psi^*(\vec p,t)\widetilde\psi(\vec q,t)
e^{i(\vec q-\vec p)\cdot\vec x}.
$$
Exchanging $\vec p,\vec q$ shows that $\vec j^*=\vec j$: it is a real probability current and satisfies the continuity equation. But the Fourier transform of the kernel is not a polynomial, so this current is nonlocal.

In fact we may also add to the probability current a part with vanishing divergence, so the choice above is not unique. This freedom cannot, however, produce a local bilinear current: if $\widetilde{\vec K}(-\vec p,\vec q)$ were a polynomial, then $(\vec p-\vec q)\cdot\widetilde{\vec K}(-\vec p,\vec q)$ would also have to be a polynomial, whereas the difference of square roots on the right-hand side of the continuity equation is not. This contradiction shows that, for the density $|\psi|^2$, there is no translation-invariant bilinear probability current satisfying the locality condition above.

So the Salpeter equation does conserve total probability, and it does have a probability current, but that current is not local, which brings us back to the problem discussed in the main text.
