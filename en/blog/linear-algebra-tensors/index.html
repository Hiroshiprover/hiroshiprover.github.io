A Tensor Formulation of Linear Algebra

October 12, 2026 · 35 min read

For Mathematicians, it's OK to skip this chapter. Nevertheless, it's recommanded to at least read Einstein's summation convention and Basis transformation.

1. Linear Algebra​

We assume the reader already has some basic familiarity with linear algebra, such as the definitions of vector spaces and bases.

There are several concepts that may not appear in an introductory linear algebra course. For those, I will state the basic definitions here. For more detailed discussions, one may consult Linear Algebra Done Right or any standard advanced linear algebra textbook.

Readers may notice that some notation conventions differ from those used in other areas. In most cases they refer to exactly the same mathematical objects, so there is no reason to panic.

Vector Spaces​

In linear algebra, every finite-dimensional vector space admits a basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​}, such that any vector can be expanded with respect to this basis.

Index conventions

Readers may be more familiar with using i,j,ki,j,ki,j,k as indices, or explicitly writing 1,2,3,…1,2,3,\dots1,2,3,….

In differential geometry, Greek letters μ,ν,ρ,…\mu,\nu,\rho,\dotsμ,ν,ρ,… are typically used for vectors and their components. This is purely a convention.

In the context of general relativity (four-dimensional spacetime), one usually assumes:

μ,ν,ρ,⋯=0,1,2,3,\mu,\nu,\rho,\dots = 0,1,2,3,μ,ν,ρ,⋯=0,1,2,3,

where 000 denotes the time component.

Meanwhile, due to historical convention, one often writes

i,j,k,⋯=1,2,3i,j,k,\dots = 1,2,3i,j,k,⋯=1,2,3

for spatial indices.

Ordinary Roman letters such as a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c are often used as abstract indices. They do not take numerical values, but only indicate tensor type. This is a somewhat subtle topic which we will discuss later.

Once a basis is chosen, any vector can be expanded as

v=∑μvμeμ.v=\sum_\mu v^\mu e_\mu.v=μ∑​vμeμ​.

Notice that vector components are written with upper indices here. This is a common convention in differential geometry, and later we will see that it is actually quite natural.

Einstein Summation Convention​

So far everything is just standard linear algebra.

Now we begin preparing for differential geometry. In differential geometry, expressions with four or more summation signs frequently appear (for example, the full contraction of the Riemann curvature tensor into the Ricci scalar). Writing out every summation sign quickly becomes visually exhausting.

To avoid this, we introduce the Einstein summation convention.

Einstein summation convention

Whenever an upper and lower index with the same symbol appears repeatedly, summation over that index is implied.

For example,

vμeμ=∑μvμeμ,v^\mu e_\mu = \sum_\mu v^\mu e_\mu,vμeμ​=μ∑​vμeμ​,

or

Aμνvν≡∑νAμνvν.A^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu \equiv \sum_\nu A^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.Aμν​vν≡ν∑​Aμν​vν.

At the moment, we do not need to worry too much about what all these symbols mean. Their meaning will become clearer later.

For now, one may simply view this as a symbolic rule.

note

Physicists often suppress the basis entirely and define vectors directly through their components vμv^\muvμ, together with transformation laws.

This approach is often more intuitive, but somewhat less structural.

Here we adopt a more mathematical viewpoint, which is useful when discussing invariants and canonical objects.

In particular, we emphasize:

  1. vμv^\muvμ are the components of the vector vvv in the basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​};
  2. {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} are actual vectors.

Dual Vector Spaces​

We first define dual vectors.

Dual vector

A dual vector is a linear functional defined on a vector space VVV:

ω:V→R,v↦ω(v).\omega: V\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad v\mapsto\omega(v).ω:V→R,v↦ω(v).

It satisfies linearity:

ω(αv+βw)=αω(v)+βω(w),∀v,w∈V.\omega(\alpha v+\beta w) = \alpha\omega(v)+\beta\omega(w), \qquad \forall v,w\in V.ω(αv+βw)=αω(v)+βω(w),∀v,w∈V.
note

If you are more comfortable with matrix language, you may think of this as a row vector.

Addition and scalar multiplication are naturally defined for dual vectors:

  1. Addition:

    (ω1+ω2)(v)=ω1(v)+ω2(v);(\omega_1+\omega_2)(v)=\omega_1(v)+\omega_2(v);(ω1​+ω2​)(v)=ω1​(v)+ω2​(v);

  2. Scalar multiplication:

    (c⋅ω)(v)=c⋅ω(v).(c \cdot \omega)(v)=c\cdot \omega(v).(c⋅ω)(v)=c⋅ω(v).

Under these operations, the set of all dual vectors forms a vector space, denoted by V∗V^*V∗.

Since dual vectors form a vector space, we naturally ask for its basis.

Suppose {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} is a basis of VVV. We define the dual basis as follows.

Dual basis

Given a basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} of VVV, define {eμ}\{e^\mu\}{eμ} in V∗V^*V∗ by

eμ(eν)=δμν.e^\mu(e_\nu)=\delta^\mu{}_\nu.eμ(eν​)=δμν​.
note

Here δμν\delta^\mu{}_\nuδμν​ is the well-known Kronecker delta:

δμν={1,μ=ν,0,μ≠ν.\delta^\mu{}_\nu= \begin{cases} 1,&\mu=\nu,\\ 0,&\mu\neq\nu. \end{cases}δμν​={1,0,​μ=ν,μ=ν.​

In differential geometry it is written with one upper and one lower index, which makes it behave naturally as the identity matrix.

It satisfies

δμνvν=vμ.\delta^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu=v^\mu.δμν​vν=vμ.

That is, it effectively replaces indices.

note

One should verify that {eμ}\{e^\mu\}{eμ} is indeed a basis, meaning that any ω∈V∗\omega\in V^*ω∈V∗ can be expanded as

ω=ωμeμ.\omega=\omega_\mu e^\mu.ω=ωμ​eμ.

Again we are using Einstein summation convention.

Notice that vector components carry upper indices, while dual vector components carry lower indices.

This is not decorative notation. It reflects different transformation laws, which we will later derive.

From this definition, one immediately obtains an important fact:

dim⁡V=dim⁡V∗\dim V=\dim V^*dimV=dimV∗

for finite-dimensional vector spaces.

note

Many linear algebra textbooks write the dual basis as eμ∗e_\mu^*eμ∗​.

Differential geometry usually writes it simply as eμe^\mueμ, which is more convenient once tensors are introduced.

The Dual of the Dual: Natural Isomorphism​

Since V∗V^*V∗ is itself a vector space, we can define its dual space V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗.

This process can clearly be repeated indefinitely, which naturally raises suspicion: do we really need to keep dualizing forever?

Fortunately, there is a canonical relation between VVV and V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗.

Natural isomorphism between VVV and V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗

For any v∈Vv\in Vv∈V, define a linear functional on V∗V^*V∗ by

v′(ω)=ω(v).v'(\omega)=\omega(v).v′(ω)=ω(v).

But linear functionals on V∗V^*V∗ are precisely elements of V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗.

Therefore every vector in VVV naturally determines an element of V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗.

In other words,

V≅V∗∗.V\cong V^{**}.V≅V∗∗.

This isomorphism does not depend on any choice of basis, so it is called a natural isomorphism or canonical isomorphism.

This allows vectors and dual vectors to pair naturally:

ω(v)=ωμvμ.\omega(v)=\omega_\mu v^\mu.ω(v)=ωμ​vμ.

Similarly,

v(ω)=vμωμ.v(\omega)=v^\mu\omega_\mu.v(ω)=vμωμ​.

Hence,

ωμvμ=vμωμ.\omega_\mu v^\mu=v^\mu\omega_\mu.ωμ​vμ=vμωμ​.

This may look trivial, but it illustrates an important principle:

as long as index structure is correct, components may be manipulated like ordinary scalars, without constantly worrying about compositions of maps.

This is one reason why Einstein summation notation is so powerful.

note

If the equations above seem mysterious, write the bases explicitly and examine the interaction between basis vectors and dual basis vectors.

Remember that components are ordinary real numbers, while dual vectors are linear maps.

warning

All discussion above assumes finite-dimensional vector spaces.

For infinite-dimensional spaces, V∗V^*V∗ is typically larger than VVV, and V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗ is usually larger than VVV as well.

Thus in general,

V≇V∗∗.V\not\cong V^{**}.V≅V∗∗.

This is one reason why quantum mechanics, despite involving vectors (kets), dual vectors (bras), and tensors (operators), usually does not directly use Einstein summation convention.

In particular, one must be careful with states such as ∣x⟩|x\rangle∣x⟩ and ∣p⟩|p\rangle∣p⟩.

Interested readers may look into rigged Hilbert spaces.

2. Tensors​

In matrix language, vectors are represented as column vectors, while covectors (another name for dual vectors) are represented as row vectors. Then what exactly is a matrix? We will use this question as motivation to introduce the definition of tensors.

Motivation​

Let us first consider the so-called quadratic form:

xTAx=[x1x2⋯xn][a11a12⋯a1na21a22⋯a2n⋮⋮⋱⋮an1an2⋯ann][x1x2⋮xn].x^TAx=\begin{bmatrix} x_1&x_2&\cdots&x_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a^1{}_1&a^1{}_2&\cdots&a^1{}_n \\ a^2{}_1&a^2{}_2&\cdots&a^2{}_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a^n{}_1&a^n{}_2&\cdots&a^n{}_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x^1\\x^2\\ \vdots \\ x^n \end{bmatrix} .xTAx=[x1​​x2​​⋯​xn​​]​a11​a21​⋮an1​​a12​a22​⋮an2​​⋯⋯⋱⋯​a1n​a2n​⋮ann​​​​x1x2⋮xn​​.

Notice that the object on the left is a covector (row vector), while the object on the right is a vector (column vector), and the entire quadratic form evaluates to a real number.

Therefore, we may think of a matrix as a map sending a covector and a vector to a real number.

note

Of course, we also know that matrices can represent linear maps between vector spaces, or changes of basis. We will return to this perspective later.

More generally, let us allow different vectors on the two sides of the quadratic form. We then observe that matrices are linear in both the covector and vector arguments:

(αxT+βyT)Az=αxTAz+βyTAz,xTA(αy+βz)=αxTAy+βxTAz.(\alpha x^T + \beta y^T)Az = \alpha x^TAz + \beta y^TAz, \qquad x^TA(\alpha y + \beta z) = \alpha x^TAy + \beta x^TAz.(αxT+βyT)Az=αxTAz+βyTAz,xTA(αy+βz)=αxTAy+βxTAz.

Although this observation may seem elementary, it suggests a more general viewpoint: rather than viewing a matrix merely as a map from one vector space to another, we may regard it as a bilinear map defined on a vector space VVV and its dual space V∗V^*V∗.

This naturally leads us to more general constructions.

Tensor​

Let us first define bilinear maps rigorously, or more generally, multilinear maps.

Multilinear Map

Let V1,V2,⋯ ,VnV_1, V_2, \cdots, V_nV1​,V2​,⋯,Vn​ be vector spaces over R\mathbb RR (or more generally, over the same field). An nnn-linear map is a map

T:V1×V2×⋯×Vn→R,T(v1,v2,⋯ ,vn)=c∈R,T: V_1 \times V_2 \times \cdots \times V_n \to \mathbb R, \qquad T(v_1, v_2, \cdots, v_n)=c\in\mathbb R,T:V1​×V2​×⋯×Vn​→R,T(v1​,v2​,⋯,vn​)=c∈R,

such that for all vi,wi∈Viv_i,w_i\in V_ivi​,wi​∈Vi​, i=1,2,⋯ ,ni=1,2,\cdots,ni=1,2,⋯,n, and all α,β∈R\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Rα,β∈R,

T(⋯ ,αvi+βwi,⋯ )=αT(⋯ ,vi,⋯ )+βT(⋯ ,wi,⋯ ).T(\cdots,\alpha v_i+\beta w_i,\cdots) = \alpha T(\cdots,v_i,\cdots) + \beta T(\cdots,w_i,\cdots).T(⋯,αvi​+βwi​,⋯)=αT(⋯,vi​,⋯)+βT(⋯,wi​,⋯).
Cartesian Product

The product symbol between vector spaces denotes the Cartesian product. The Cartesian product simply collects elements from two spaces into ordered pairs:

V×W:={(v,w)∣v∈V, w∈W}.V\times W:=\{(v,w)\mid v\in V,\ w\in W\}.V×W:={(v,w)∣v∈V, w∈W}.

Note that "ordered" means (v,w)≠(w,v)(v,w)\neq (w,v)(v,w)=(w,v) in general.

In particular, for repeated Cartesian products of the same vector space, we use exponential notation:

Vn:=V×V×⋯×V⏟n times.V^n:=\underbrace{V\times V\times\cdots\times V}_{n\text{ times}}.Vn:=n timesV×V×⋯×V​​.

This is also the origin of the familiar notation Rn\mathbb R^nRn.

Cartesian products satisfy various basic properties. For example, the Cartesian product of two vector spaces is again a vector space, and the Cartesian product of two subspaces is isomorphic to their direct sum. We will not discuss these facts in detail here.

More generally, Cartesian products can be defined for arbitrary sets, not only vector spaces.

We can now define tensors in a simple way:

Tensor

A tensor of type (k,l)(k,l)(k,l) is a multilinear map defined on V∗k×Vl{V^*}^k\times V^lV∗k×Vl. Equivalently, a (k,l)(k,l)(k,l)-tensor is a multilinear map that takes kkk covectors and lll vectors as inputs, and outputs a real number.

note

You may wonder why the dual space is placed before the vector space. In fact, this is because we are considering the objects on which tensors act, i.e. the domain of the tensor. If we consider what a tensor actually is, we will find that the front becomes vectors and the back becomes covectors. Specifically, any tensor can be written as:

T=Tμ1⋯μkν1⋯νl eμ1⊗⋯⊗eμk⊗eν1⊗⋯⊗eνl,T = T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_k}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_l} \, e_{\mu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{\mu_k} \otimes e^{\nu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{\nu_l},T=Tμ1​⋯μk​ν1​⋯νl​​eμ1​​⊗⋯⊗eμk​​⊗eν1​⊗⋯⊗eνl​,

Here, {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} is the basis of the vector space VVV, while {eν}\{e^\nu\}{eν} is the dual basis.

note

A covector maps a vector to a real number, while a vector may also be viewed as mapping a covector to a real number. Therefore, under our definition, covectors and vectors can be regarded as tensors of type (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) respectively. In particular, a scalar (real number) is called a (0,0)(0,0)(0,0)-tensor.

Tensor Addition and Scalar Multiplication​

From the definition above, we can naturally define tensor addition and scalar multiplication:

  1. (T+S)(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl)=T(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl)+S(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl),(T+S)(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l)=T(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l)+S(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l),(T+S)(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​)=T(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​)+S(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​),

  2. (αT)(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl)=α⋅T(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl).(\alpha T)(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l)=\alpha\cdot T(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l).(αT)(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​)=α⋅T(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​).

These definitions require tensors to have the same type in order to be added or subtracted. This is natural, since there is no meaningful way to add a matrix and a vector directly.

Under these operations, all tensors of type (k,l)(k,l)(k,l) naturally form a vector space, denoted by TV(k,l)\mathscr T_V(k,l)TV​(k,l).

If the base space VVV has dimension nnn, then

dim⁡TV(k,l)=nk+l.\dim \mathscr T_V(k,l)=n^{k+l}.dimTV​(k,l)=nk+l.

Although this formula looks obvious, proving it rigorously is somewhat tedious. Interested readers may try constructing an explicit basis using a basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} of VVV together with its dual basis.

Tensor Product​

We begin with some motivation from matrices.

Consider the following fact:

[x1x2⋮xn][y1y2⋯yn]=[x1y1x1y2⋯x1ynx2y1x2y2⋯x2yn⋮⋮⋱⋮xny1xny2⋯xnyn].\begin{bmatrix} x^1\\x^2\\ \vdots \\ x^n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y_1&y_2&\cdots&y_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x^1y_1&x^1y_2&\cdots&x^1y_n \\ x^2y_1&x^2y_2&\cdots&x^2y_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x^ny_1&x^ny_2&\cdots&x^ny_n \end{bmatrix}.​x1x2⋮xn​​[y1​​y2​​⋯​yn​​]=​x1y1​x2y1​⋮xny1​​x1y2​x2y2​⋮xny2​​⋯⋯⋱⋯​x1yn​x2yn​⋮xnyn​​​.

In other words, the tensor product of a (1,0)(1,0)(1,0)-tensor and a (0,1)(0,1)(0,1)-tensor is a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor.

Of course, the definition of tensor product itself does not depend on matrices. This motivates the following formal definition.

Tensor Product

Let TTT be a tensor of type (k,l)(k,l)(k,l) and SSS be a tensor of type (k′,l′)(k',l')(k′,l′). Their tensor product is defined to be a tensor of type (k+k′,l+l′)(k+k',l+l')(k+k′,l+l′):

(T⊗S)(ω1,⋯ ,ωk,ωk+1,⋯ ,ωk+k′;v1,⋯ ,vl,vl+1,⋯ ,vl+l′):=  T(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl) S(ωk+1,⋯ ,ωk+k′;vl+1,⋯ ,vl+l′).\begin{aligned} &(T\otimes S)(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k,\omega^{k+1},\cdots,\omega^{k+k'};v_1,\cdots,v_l,v_{l+1},\cdots,v_{l+l'}) \\ :=\;& T(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l)\, S(\omega^{k+1},\cdots,\omega^{k+k'};v_{l+1},\cdots,v_{l+l'}). \end{aligned}:=​(T⊗S)(ω1,⋯,ωk,ωk+1,⋯,ωk+k′;v1​,⋯,vl​,vl+1​,⋯,vl+l′​)T(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​)S(ωk+1,⋯,ωk+k′;vl+1​,⋯,vl+l′​).​

Although this expression looks intimidating, the idea is simple: feed the first group of arguments into TTT, the second group into SSS, and multiply the resulting real numbers.

Note that tensor products are generally not commutative, since the order of vector and covector inputs matters.

note

There is a special case: the tensor product of a vector and a covector may be rearranged without ambiguity, since each contributes only one slot.

For example,

(eμ⊗eν)⊗(eρ⊗eσ)=eμ⊗eν⊗eρ⊗eσ≡eμ⊗eρ⊗eσ⊗eν.(e_\mu\otimes e^\nu)\otimes(e_\rho\otimes e_\sigma) = e_\mu\otimes e^\nu\otimes e_\rho\otimes e_\sigma \equiv e_\mu\otimes e_\rho\otimes e_\sigma\otimes e^\nu.(eμ​⊗eν)⊗(eρ​⊗eσ​)=eμ​⊗eν⊗eρ​⊗eσ​≡eμ​⊗eρ​⊗eσ​⊗eν.

This is why we may naturally regard the result of tensoring a (k,l)(k,l)(k,l)-tensor and a (k′,l′)(k',l')(k′,l′)-tensor as a (k+k′,l+l′)(k+k',l+l')(k+k′,l+l′)-tensor rather than something strange, say (k,l;k′,l′)(k, l; k', l')(k,l;k′,l′) or other things.

Addition and scalar multiplication interact naturally with tensor products:

  1. T⊗S+T′⊗S=(T+T′)⊗S,T\otimes S + T'\otimes S = (T+T')\otimes S,T⊗S+T′⊗S=(T+T′)⊗S,

  2. T⊗S+T⊗S′=T⊗(S+S′),T\otimes S + T\otimes S' = T\otimes(S+S'),T⊗S+T⊗S′=T⊗(S+S′),

  3. α(T⊗S)=(αT)⊗S=T⊗(αS).\alpha(T\otimes S) = (\alpha T)\otimes S = T\otimes(\alpha S).α(T⊗S)=(αT)⊗S=T⊗(αS).

Tensor Product of Spaces

The finite linear combinations of elementary tensors v⊗wv\otimes wv⊗w form a new vector space:

V⊗W:=span⁡R{v⊗w∣v∈V, w∈W}.V\otimes W := \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\{v\otimes w\mid v\in V,\ w\in W\}.V⊗W:=spanR​{v⊗w∣v∈V, w∈W}.

An arbitrary element is a finite sum ∑ivi⊗wi\sum_i v_i\otimes w_i∑i​vi​⊗wi​ and need not be a single elementary tensor.

If dim⁡V=m\dim V=mdimV=m and dim⁡W=n\dim W=ndimW=n, then dim⁡(V⊗W)=mn.\dim(V\otimes W)=mn.dim(V⊗W)=mn. We omit the proof of this fact.

note

For a tensor product generating a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor, we have

A=v⊗ω=(vμeμ)⊗(ωνeν)=vμων eμ⊗eν.A=v\otimes\omega = (v^\mu e_\mu)\otimes(\omega_\nu e^\nu) = v^\mu\omega_\nu\,e_\mu\otimes e^\nu.A=v⊗ω=(vμeμ​)⊗(ων​eν)=vμων​eμ​⊗eν.

Define

Aμν:=vμων.A^\mu{}_\nu:=v^\mu\omega_\nu.Aμν​:=vμων​.

These are precisely the components of a matrix.

Of course, not every tensor can be written as a single tensor product of this form. In general, one needs linear combinations of such elementary tensors.

note

Mathematicians often prefer a more abstract definition of tensor product using a universal property.

Fix a vector space UUU and a bilinear map

g:V×W→U.g:V\times W\to U.g:V×W→U.

Require that, for every real vector space ZZZ and every bilinear map f:V×W→Zf:V\times W\to Zf:V×W→Z, there exists a unique linear map h:U→Zh:U\to Zh:U→Z such that

f=h∘g.f=h\circ g.f=h∘g.

Here (U,g)(U,g)(U,g) is fixed; it is the unique map hhh that depends on fff.

We then define the tensor product space (up to isomorphism) by

V⊗W:=U.V\otimes W:=U.V⊗W:=U.

The elementary tensor v⊗wv\otimes wv⊗w is g(v,w)g(v,w)g(v,w). Any two pairs satisfying this property are related by a unique isomorphism compatible with their bilinear maps.

This definition is elegant but useless, ONLY Mathematicians CAN DO. It does not directly tell us how to construct the space. Since our focus is differential geometry and explicit computation, we only mention this viewpoint briefly.

Contraction​

We again begin with matrices. A matrix acts on a vector and produces another vector. In tensor language, this means that a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor can combine with a (1,0)(1,0)(1,0)-tensor to produce a new (1,0)(1,0)(1,0)-tensor.

Write a matrix as A=Aμν eμ⊗eν,A=A^\mu{}_\nu\,e_\mu\otimes e^\nu,A=Aμν​eμ​⊗eν, which is a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor. Now, let v=vρeρv=v^\rho e_\rhov=vρeρ​ be a vector, in linear algebra, matrix multiplication produces a new vector:

(Aμνvν)eμ=wμeμ.(A^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu)e_\mu = w^\mu e_\mu.(Aμν​vν)eμ​=wμeμ​.

This shows that after combining a matrix and a vector, one vector-covector pair is "eliminated", leaving only one vector basis. How does this happen?

Well, the most basic way to combine tensors is first to take their tensor product, therefore we consider:

A⊗v=Aμνvρ eμ⊗eν⊗eρ,A\otimes v = A^\mu{}_\nu v^\rho\, e_\mu\otimes e^\nu\otimes e_\rho,A⊗v=Aμν​vρeμ​⊗eν⊗eρ​,

which is a (2,1)(2,1)(2,1)-tensor, still not a vector. Hence we need one further operation: pair one upper index with one lower index and sum over them. This is what we call a contraction:

Contraction Operator

Define

Cji:TV(k,l)→TV(k−1,l−1),C^i_j:\mathscr T_V(k,l)\to\mathscr T_V(k-1,l-1),Cji​:TV​(k,l)→TV​(k−1,l−1),

which contracts the iii-th upper index with the jjj-th lower index.

Explicitly,

Cji(T)=T(⋅,…,eμ,…,⋅;⋅,…,eμ,…,⋅),C^i_j(T) = T(\cdot,\dots,e^\mu,\dots,\cdot;\cdot,\dots,e_\mu,\dots,\cdot),Cji​(T)=T(⋅,…,eμ,…,⋅;⋅,…,eμ​,…,⋅),

where eμe^\mueμ is inserted into the iii-th covector slot, eμe_\mueμ​ into the jjj-th vector slot, and we sum over μ\muμ. Equivalently, contraction uses the fact that eμ(eν)=δμνe^\mu(e_\nu)=\delta^\mu{}_\nueμ(eν​)=δμν​ to eliminate one upper-lower index pair.

Back to our matrix-vector example, we contract the tensor product:

C12(A⊗v)=C12(Aμνvρ eμ⊗eν⊗eρ).C^2_1(A\otimes v) = C^2_1\left( A^\mu{}_\nu v^\rho\, e_\mu\otimes e^\nu\otimes e_\rho \right).C12​(A⊗v)=C12​(Aμν​vρeμ​⊗eν⊗eρ​).

Contracting the second upper index with the first lower index gives

Aμνvνeμ,A^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu e_\mu,Aμν​vνeμ​,

which is exactly the expected result of matrix multiplication.

Thus, matrix multiplication is, in tensor language, tensor product + contraction. This idea generalizes to arbitrary tensors. Although in general, there is no unique multiplication rule between tensors, we can always first take tensor products, and then specify which pairs of upper and lower indices should be contracted. Different contraction choices usually lead to different results.

For example, in the same setting, we do a different contraction:

C11(A⊗v)=Aμμvνeν,C^1_1(A\otimes v)=A^\mu{}_\mu v^\nu e_\nu,C11​(A⊗v)=Aμμ​vνeν​,

which is simply gives the trace of the matrix times the vector, where the trace of a matrix is defined as:

tr(A)=Aμμ.\mathrm{tr}(A)=A^\mu{}_\mu.tr(A)=Aμμ​.

Thus, contraction can be understood as one of the most fundamental interaction rules between tensors: it lowers tensor rank by pairing an upper index with a lower index.

note

For example,

3. More about indices: basis transformation​

We now have enough basic machinery to answer a very fundamental question: why are the components of a vector written with upper indices, while those of a covector use lower indices, and why is it the opposite for basis vectors? This entire “tragedy” ultimately comes from physicists naming things according to their behavior under basis transformations.

Basis Vectors​

Consider a linear map

Λ:V→V,{eμ}↦{eμ′},\Lambda: V \to V, \quad \{e_\mu \} \mapsto \{e'_\mu\},Λ:V→V,{eμ​}↦{eμ′​},

This is clearly a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor (a matrix), and we have

eμ′=Λνμeν.e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu.eμ′​=Λνμ​eν​.

Here both eμe_\mueμ​ and eμ′e'_\mueμ′​ are actual vectors. Note that Λνμ\Lambda^\nu{}_\muΛνμ​ are the components of Λ\LambdaΛ with respect to the basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​}.

note

The symbol Λ\LambdaΛ is often used to denote a special class of transformations: Lorentz transformations, which arise from the requirement that the spacetime interval ds2\mathrm{d}s^2ds2 is invariant under coordinate changes. In our discussion, however, no such restriction is imposed.

Vector Components​

Next we analyze how the components of a vector transform under the basis change Λ\LambdaΛ. Consider a vector vvv written in two different bases:

v=vμeμ=v′μeμ′.v = v^\mu e_\mu = v'^\mu e'_\mu.v=vμeμ​=v′μeμ′​.

Substituting the basis transformation gives

v′μΛνμeν=vνeν,v'^\mu \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu = v^\nu e_\nu,v′μΛνμ​eν​=vνeν​,

hence

v′μ=(Λ−1)μνvν.v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.v′μ=(Λ−1)μν​vν.
note

In tensor language, the inverse of a matrix is a tensor satisfying

Λμρ(Λ−1)ρν=δμν.\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho (\Lambda^{-1})^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu.Λμρ​(Λ−1)ρν​=δμν​.

Dual Basis​

Starting from the definition of the dual basis,

δμν=eμ(eν),\delta^\mu{}_\nu = e^\mu(e_\nu),δμν​=eμ(eν​),

we multiply both sides by Λνρe′ρ\Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e'^\rhoΛνρ​e′ρ, obtaining

Λμρe′ρ=eμ(e′ρΛνρeν)=eμ(e′ρeρ′)=eμ.\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho e'^\rho = e^\mu(e'^\rho \Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e_\nu) = e^\mu(e'^\rho e'_\rho) = e^\mu.Λμρ​e′ρ=eμ(e′ρΛνρ​eν​)=eμ(e′ρeρ′​)=eμ.

Multiplying by (Λ−1)σμ(\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu(Λ−1)σμ​ yields

e′σ=(Λ−1)σμeμ.e'^\sigma = (\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu e^\mu.e′σ=(Λ−1)σμ​eμ.

Renaming dummy indices gives

e′μ=(Λ−1)μνeν.e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu.e′μ=(Λ−1)μν​eν.

Covector Components​

Similarly, starting from

ω=ωμeμ=ωμ′e′μ,\omega = \omega_\mu e^\mu = \omega'_\mu e'^\mu,ω=ωμ​eμ=ωμ′​e′μ,

and substituting the basis transformation, we obtain

ωμ′=Λνμων.\omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu.ωμ′​=Λνμ​ων​.

Contravariance and Covariance​

We now observe a unified pattern:

Contravariance and Covariance

The transformations of basis vectors and covector components are

eμ′=Λνμeν,ωμ′=Λνμων,e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu, \qquad \omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu,eμ′​=Λνμ​eν​,ωμ′​=Λνμ​ων​,

while the transformations of dual basis vectors and vector components are

e′μ=(Λ−1)μνeν,v′μ=(Λ−1)μνvν.e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu, \qquad v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.e′μ=(Λ−1)μν​eν,v′μ=(Λ−1)μν​vν.

We see that any object carrying an upper index transforms with the inverse of the basis transformation, while any object with a lower index transforms with the transformation itself.

Thus, upper indices are called contravariant indices, and lower indices are called covariant indices; vectors are contravariant, and covectors are covariant (which is why dual vectors are also called covectors).

This explains why Einstein’s summation convention is defined in this way: indices do not merely indicate tensor type, but rather encode transformation behavior. Hence vectors use upper indices and covectors use lower indices, while repeated upper–lower pairs indicate summation, i.e. contraction, describing how tensors interact.

Abstract Index Notation​

At the end of the previous section we made the point that the position of an index does not declare the tensor type, but rather the transformation behavior. If we want to declare the tensor type, our best available language is algebraic: naming the spaces and maps, that is, the long expression T(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl)T(\omega^1, \cdots, \omega^k; v_1, \cdots, v_l)T(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​). Objects of that size are terrifying to manipulate. To merely declare the type, of course, we can say “a (k,l)(k,l)(k,l)-tensor”, but such a declaration cannot be carried along during a computation; if at every step we had to open a new line and explain the type in words, the text would become very long.

Is there a notation that both participates in computations naturally and declares tensor types naturally? This is what abstract index notation provides. Specifically, we agree to use Roman letters a,b,c,⋯a, b, c, \cdotsa,b,c,⋯ to declare tensor types, and Greek letters μ,ν,ρ,⋯\mu, \nu, \rho, \cdotsμ,ν,ρ,⋯ for concrete indices and summation.

For example, basis vectors and dual basis vectors are now written

(eμ)a,(eν)b,(e_\mu)^a, \qquad (e^\nu)_b,(eμ​)a,(eν)b​,

while a general vector and covector become

va=vμ(eμ)a,ωb=ων(eν)b.v^a = v^\mu (e_\mu)^a, \qquad \omega_b = \omega_\nu (e^\nu)_b.va=vμ(eμ​)a,ωb​=ων​(eν)b​.

How does the abstract index language handle operations between tensors? Consider first the tensor product. In abstract index notation we omit the tensor product symbol, for instance

Tabcvd≡T⊗v≡Tμνρvσeμ⊗eν⊗eρ⊗eσ,T^a{}_{bc} v^d \equiv T \otimes v\equiv T^\mu{}_{\nu \rho} v^\sigma e_\mu \otimes e^\nu \otimes e^\rho \otimes e_\sigma,Tabc​vd≡T⊗v≡Tμνρ​vσeμ​⊗eν⊗eρ⊗eσ​,

Next comes contraction: we agree that a repeated upper–lower abstract index denotes a contraction, for example

or

Tabcωb≡C22(T⊗ω)=T(⋅,eμ; ⋅) ω(eμ)≡Tρμσωμeρ⊗eσ≡Tρμσωμ(eρ)a(eσ)c.T^{ab}{}_c \omega_b \equiv C^2_2(T \otimes \omega) = T(·, e^\mu; \ ·)\,\omega(e_\mu) \equiv T^{\rho \mu}{}_{\sigma} \omega_\mu e_\rho \otimes e^\sigma \equiv T^{\rho \mu}{}_{\sigma} \omega_\mu (e_\rho)^a (e^\sigma)_c.Tabc​ωb​≡C22​(T⊗ω)=T(⋅,eμ; ⋅)ω(eμ​)≡Tρμσ​ωμ​eρ​⊗eσ≡Tρμσ​ωμ​(eρ​)a(eσ)c​.

This can be a little convoluted; my advice is to verify each equality step by step. One way to read it is that abstract indices are introduced so that objects carrying no index are not mistaken for scalars.

In practice, however, we generally do not use abstract index notation. Roman letters are easily confused with the usual index conventions, especially in contexts where frames are used. For that reason I will not use it in my notes; it is only sketched here.

4. The Metric and Index Raising and Lowering​

So far, vectors (elements of VVV) and covectors (elements of V∗V^*V∗) have remained two distinct kinds of object: their indices sit at different heights, and they transform in opposite ways. Apart from the natural pairing ω(v)\omega(v)ω(v), we have no general way to turn a vector into a covector. The earlier isomorphism V≅V∗∗V\cong V^{**}V≅V∗∗ worked precisely because it needs no extra structure; a general isomorphism between VVV and V∗V^*V∗ does not exist. To let vectors and covectors be converted into one another, we must introduce an additional structure by hand: a metric.

Inner Products, Dirac Notation, and Duality​

To let the inner product produce a link between vectors and covectors, we first write the definition of an inner product carefully. We temporarily allow complex scalars here, and return to the real case at the end.

Inner Product

Let VVV be a vector space over the complex numbers C\mathbb{C}C. An inner product on VVV is a map

⟨⋅,⋅⟩:V×V→C,(u,v)↦⟨u,v⟩,\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V\times V \to \mathbb{C}, \qquad (u,v)\mapsto \langle u, v\rangle,⟨⋅,⋅⟩:V×V→C,(u,v)↦⟨u,v⟩,

satisfying the following three conditions (the conjugation convention common in physics):

⟨u,αv+βw⟩=α⟨u,v⟩+β⟨u,w⟩,⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩‾,\langle u, \alpha v + \beta w\rangle = \alpha\langle u, v\rangle + \beta\langle u, w\rangle, \qquad \langle u, v\rangle = \overline{\langle v, u\rangle},⟨u,αv+βw⟩=α⟨u,v⟩+β⟨u,w⟩,⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩​,

and ⟨v,v⟩>0\langle v, v\rangle > 0⟨v,v⟩>0 for every v≠0v\neq 0v=0.

The first condition constrains only the second argument; combined with the second, it forces the first argument to be conjugate-linear:

⟨αu+βw,v⟩=αˉ⟨u,v⟩+βˉ⟨w,v⟩.\langle \alpha u + \beta w, v\rangle = \bar\alpha\langle u, v\rangle + \bar\beta\langle w, v\rangle.⟨αu+βw,v⟩=αˉ⟨u,v⟩+βˉ​⟨w,v⟩.

Conjugate symmetry also guarantees that ⟨v,v⟩\langle v, v\rangle⟨v,v⟩ is real, which is what makes the positive-definiteness condition meaningful.

note

The mathematical literature often puts the conjugation on the second argument instead (linear in the first), opposite to our choice. We use the physics convention so that ⟨v∣w⟩\langle v|w\rangle⟨v∣w⟩ below is linear in ∣w⟩|w\rangle∣w⟩, matching the order in which Dirac notation is written.

Now split the inner product down the middle. Fix uuu, ignore the second argument for the moment, and look only at how the inner product depends on it. This gives a map

φu:V→C,φu(v)≡⟨u,v⟩.\varphi_u: V\to \mathbb{C}, \qquad \varphi_u(v) \equiv \langle u, v\rangle.φu​:V→C,φu​(v)≡⟨u,v⟩.

Because the inner product is linear in its second argument, φu\varphi_uφu​ is a linear functional on VVV, that is, a covector: φu∈V∗\varphi_u\in V^*φu​∈V∗. This is precisely Dirac's idea: the bracket can be cut into two halves, the left half ⟨u∣\langle u|⟨u∣ being the thing that merely “waits to be fed a vector”, and the right half ∣v⟩|v\rangle∣v⟩ being the vector that gets fed in; only together do they make a number. Vectors and covectors thus acquire notation of their own:

∣v⟩ ↔ v∈V,⟨v∣ ↔ φv∈V∗,⟨u∣v⟩≡⟨u,v⟩.|v\rangle\ \leftrightarrow\ v\in V, \qquad \langle v|\ \leftrightarrow\ \varphi_v\in V^*, \qquad \langle u|v\rangle \equiv \langle u, v\rangle.∣v⟩ ↔ v∈V,⟨v∣ ↔ φv​∈V∗,⟨u∣v⟩≡⟨u,v⟩.

This is where the names ket and bra come from: bra plus ket makes bracket. So the statement “the bra is the covector of the ket” carries no extra magic; it just refers to the correspondence v↦φvv\mapsto\varphi_vv↦φv​ induced by the inner product.

That correspondence is injective: if φu=φw\varphi_u=\varphi_wφu​=φw​, then ⟨u−w,v⟩=0\langle u-w, v\rangle=0⟨u−w,v⟩=0 for every vvv; taking v=u−wv=u-wv=u−w and using positive-definiteness gives u=wu=wu=w.

The Riesz Representation Theorem

Let HHH be a Hilbert space, that is, a complete inner product space. Then every continuous linear functional φ∈H∗\varphi\in H^*φ∈H∗ can be written uniquely as

φ(v)=⟨u,v⟩,∀v∈H.\varphi(v)=\langle u, v\rangle, \qquad \forall v\in H.φ(v)=⟨u,v⟩,∀v∈H.

In other words, v↦φvv\mapsto\varphi_vv↦φv​ is surjective; by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality it is also an isometry, so HHH and H∗H^*H∗ correspond one-to-one through the inner product (in the complex case the correspondence is conjugate-linear, i.e. an isometric anti-isomorphism).

The proof goes as follows. The null space null⁡φ\operatorname{null}\varphinullφ of a continuous linear functional is a closed subspace of codimension 111. Completeness makes the orthogonal decomposition available: choose a nonzero vector u0u_0u0​ in the orthogonal complement (null⁡φ)⊥(\operatorname{null}\varphi)^\perp(nullφ)⊥, and write any vvv as v=v0+λu0v=v_0+\lambda u_0v=v0​+λu0​ with v0∈null⁡φv_0\in\operatorname{null}\varphiv0​∈nullφ, where λ\lambdaλ is fixed by φ(v)=λφ(u0)\varphi(v)=\lambda\varphi(u_0)φ(v)=λφ(u0​). Since u0⊥v0u_0\perp v_0u0​⊥v0​, we have ⟨u0,v⟩=λ⟨u0,u0⟩\langle u_0, v\rangle=\lambda\langle u_0, u_0\rangle⟨u0​,v⟩=λ⟨u0​,u0​⟩, so φ(v)\varphi(v)φ(v) is proportional to ⟨u0,v⟩\langle u_0, v\rangle⟨u0​,v⟩. On the other hand φ\varphiφ vanishes on null⁡φ\operatorname{null}\varphinullφ, so the representing vector must be orthogonal to null⁡φ\operatorname{null}\varphinullφ, and hence must lie in the one-dimensional space spanned by u0u_0u0​. Multiplying u0u_0u0​ by a suitable constant (with a conjugation in the complex case) then determines the representing vector. Uniqueness follows from the injectivity proved above.

The Trouble in Infinite Dimensions: Completeness and Antiduality

Both statements above need qualification in infinite dimensions. Injectivity uses only positive-definiteness and is independent of dimension; surjectivity, however, rests on two hypotheses.

The first is completeness. If VVV is merely an inner product space and not complete, then v↦φvv\mapsto\varphi_vv↦φv​ is no longer surjective: its image is a dense proper subspace of V∗V^*V∗, and the extra functionals in the dual space have no representing vector in VVV. In fact V∗V^*V∗ is the completion of VVV (up to isometric isomorphism); to make the two sides match, one must dualize once more — an operation of antiduality in the complex case, since the correspondence is conjugate-linear — and the resulting V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗ is the side with the gap filled in, with V⊆V∗∗V\subseteq V^{**}V⊆V∗∗. The Riesz theorem says precisely that when VVV is already complete, this step produces nothing new.

The second is continuity. Discontinuous linear functionals are not in H∗H^*H∗, and the inner product cannot represent them. In quantum mechanics ∣x⟩|x\rangle∣x⟩ and ∣p⟩|p\rangle∣p⟩ are manifestations of this clash: they are not normalizable, hence do not belong to the Hilbert space HHH, and yet they must have inner products with states in HHH. The remedy is to sandwich an extra space in between: take a dense subspace Φ⊆H\Phi\subseteq HΦ⊆H of sufficiently well-behaved vectors, and place ∣x⟩|x\rangle∣x⟩, ∣p⟩|p\rangle∣p⟩ in the (anti)dual space Φ×\Phi^\timesΦ× of Φ\PhiΦ,

Φ⊆H⊆Φ×,\Phi\subseteq H\subseteq \Phi^\times,Φ⊆H⊆Φ×,

which is the rigged Hilbert space mentioned earlier. In this framework bras and kets are still the two halves of the inner product; they simply live in different spaces.

Now back to the main line of this article. Note that this bridge depends on the inner product itself: change the inner product, and v↦φvv\mapsto\varphi_vv↦φv​ changes with it. The complex inner product is also conjugate-linear, which lies beyond the language of real tensors. From here on we work only over real vector spaces.

The Metric​

Over a real vector space, an inner product takes two vectors and maps them symmetrically and bilinearly to a real number, which is exactly the definition of a (0,2)(0,2)(0,2)-tensor. So we may as well regard the inner product as an object in the tensor space, and use it to define a metric:

The Metric

A metric on a vector space VVV is a (0,2)(0,2)(0,2)-tensor ggg, that is, a bilinear map

g:V×V→R,g: V\times V \to \R,g:V×V→R,

which is symmetric, g(u,v)=g(v,u)g(u,v)=g(v,u)g(u,v)=g(v,u), and non-degenerate: if some uuu satisfies g(u,v)=0g(u,v)=0g(u,v)=0 for all v∈Vv\in Vv∈V, then u=0u=0u=0.

In components,

g(u,v)=gμνuμvν,gμν=g(eμ,eν).g(u,v)=g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu v^\nu, \qquad g_{\mu\nu}=g(e_\mu,e_\nu).g(u,v)=gμν​uμvν,gμν​=g(eμ​,eν​).

Symmetry means gμν=gνμg_{\mu\nu}=g_{\nu\mu}gμν​=gνμ​; non-degeneracy means det⁡(gμν)≠0\det(g_{\mu\nu})\neq 0det(gμν​)=0, so gμνg_{\mu\nu}gμν​ is invertible. We write its inverse as gμνg^{\mu\nu}gμν, satisfying

gμνgνρ=δμρg^{\mu\nu}g_{\nu\rho}=\delta^\mu{}_\rhogμνgνρ​=δμρ​

(as usual, repeated upper–lower indices are summed).

A metric generalizes an inner product; the difference is that positive-definiteness is no longer required. Positive-definiteness would demand g(u,u)>0g(u,u)>0g(u,u)>0 for all u≠0u\neq 0u=0, which immediately rules out the Minkowski metric used throughout relativity: the Minkowski metric is indefinite, and g(u,u)g(u,u)g(u,u) may be positive, negative, or zero. As long as we keep symmetry and non-degeneracy, we can still use the metric to raise and lower indices just as with an inner product.

Euclidean and Minkowski Metrics​

Two examples are worth writing out.

Euclidean metric. Take

gμν=δμν=diag⁡(1,1,…,1),g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\dots,1),gμν​=δμν​=diag(1,1,…,1),

so that g(u,v)=δμνuμvν=∑μuμvμg(u,v)=\delta_{\mu\nu}u^\mu v^\nu=\sum_\mu u^\mu v^\mug(u,v)=δμν​uμvν=∑μ​uμvμ, the usual dot product.

Minkowski metric. Relativity conventionally uses the (−+++)(-+++)(−+++) signature:

gμν=ημν=diag⁡(−1,1,1,1),g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1),gμν​=ημν​=diag(−1,1,1,1),

so that

g(u,v)=−u0v0+u1v1+u2v2+u3v3.g(u,v)=-u^0v^0+u^1v^1+u^2v^2+u^3v^3.g(u,v)=−u0v0+u1v1+u2v2+u3v3.

The spacetime interval is precisely this metric acting on infinitesimal displacements:

ds2=ημν dxμdxν=−dt2+dx2+dy2+dz2.\mathrm{d} s^2=\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{d} x^\mu \mathrm{d} x^\nu =-\mathrm{d} t^2+\mathrm{d} x^2+\mathrm{d} y^2+\mathrm{d} z^2.ds2=ημν​dxμdxν=−dt2+dx2+dy2+dz2.

Because g00=−1g_{00}=-1g00​=−1, the time direction enters with a minus sign; this sign is the essential difference between an indefinite metric and the Euclidean one, and it is why “squared length” can be negative in Minkowski spacetime.

warning

In general relativity the metric is no longer a fixed constant matrix but a dynamical field gμν(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x)gμν​(x) on spacetime, determined by the distribution of matter through the Einstein field equations,

Gμν=8πG TμνG_{\mu\nu}=8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}Gμν​=8πGTμν​

(in natural units, c=1c=1c=1). General relativity therefore contains a great many different metrics: the field outside a star, a black hole, an expanding universe all correspond to different choices of gμνg_{\mu\nu}gμν​. Specifying a metric specifies an inner-product structure, and hence a geometry.

Raising and Lowering Indices: the Musical Isomorphism​

With a metric in hand, indices can be moved up and down. We define

Vμ≡gμνVν,Vμ≡gμνVν.V_\mu\equiv g_{\mu\nu}V^\nu, \qquad V^\mu\equiv g^{\mu\nu}V_\nu.Vμ​≡gμν​Vν,Vμ≡gμνVν​.

The first turns a vector into a covector, the second turns a covector back into a vector. The two are consistent because gμνg^{\mu\nu}gμν is the inverse of gμνg_{\mu\nu}gμν​:

gμνVν=gμνgνρVρ=δμρVρ=Vμ.g^{\mu\nu}V_\nu=g^{\mu\nu}g_{\nu\rho}V^\rho=\delta^\mu{}_\rho V^\rho=V^\mu.gμνVν​=gμνgνρ​Vρ=δμρ​Vρ=Vμ.

In tensor language, lowering an index is just a contraction with the metric: gμνVνg_{\mu\nu}V^\nugμν​Vν is the contraction of g⊗Vg\otimes Vg⊗V over one upper–lower pair. For a higher-rank tensor we simply repeat the operation on each index to be changed, for instance

Tμν=gνρTμρ,Tμν=gμρgνσTρσ.T^\mu{}_\nu=g_{\nu\rho}T^{\mu\rho}, \qquad T_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}T^{\rho\sigma}.Tμν​=gνρ​Tμρ,Tμν​=gμρ​gνσ​Tρσ.

Note that indices can only be raised or lowered with the metric (or its inverse); otherwise the tensor type cannot change. The distinction between upper and lower indices remains — the metric merely provides a bridge between them.

What, then, does this bridge actually do? For a fixed metric, define

♭:V→V∗,v↦g(v, ⋅ ),\flat: V\to V^*, \qquad v\mapsto g(v,\ \cdot\,),♭:V→V∗,v↦g(v, ⋅),

that is, (♭v)(w)≡g(v,w)(\flat v)(w)\equiv g(v,w)(♭v)(w)≡g(v,w); in components (♭v)μ=gμνvν(\flat v)_\mu=g_{\mu\nu}v^\nu(♭v)μ​=gμν​vν, which is exactly lowering an index. Conversely, define

♯:V∗→V,ω↦g−1(ω, ⋅ ),\sharp: V^*\to V, \qquad \omega\mapsto g^{-1}(\omega,\ \cdot\,),♯:V∗→V,ω↦g−1(ω, ⋅),

with components (♯ω)μ=gμνων(\sharp\omega)^\mu=g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\nu(♯ω)μ=gμνων​, which is exactly raising an index.

The map ♭\flat♭ is linear because ggg is linear in its first argument. It is also injective: if ♭v=0\flat v=0♭v=0, then g(v,w)=0g(v,w)=0g(v,w)=0 for all www, and non-degeneracy forces v=0v=0v=0. In finite dimensions injective implies bijective, and dim⁡V=dim⁡V∗\dim V=\dim V^*dimV=dimV∗, so ♭\flat♭ is an isomorphism with inverse ♯\sharp♯. Hence

V≅V∗.V\cong V^*.V≅V∗.

This isomorphism is called the musical isomorphism. The name comes from notation: the flat sign ♭\flat♭ lowers a pitch, just as it lowers an index, while the sharp sign ♯\sharp♯ raises one. The maps ♭\flat♭ and ♯\sharp♯ act on exactly the two kinds of index that the previous section took pains to distinguish.

warning

The phrase “natural isomorphism” needs care here. The construction of ♭\flat♭ depends on the metric itself: change the metric, and ♭\flat♭ changes with it. So V≅V∗V\cong V^*V≅V∗ is not canonical in the sense of the earlier V≅V∗∗V\cong V^{**}V≅V∗∗, which requires no choice; it is natural only once a metric has been fixed. In other words, V≅V∗∗V\cong V^{**}V≅V∗∗ is a genuinely natural isomorphism in the category of abstract vector spaces, whereas V≅V∗V\cong V^*V≅V∗ needs the extra structure of a metric. Physically: without a specified metric, vectors and covectors remain two distinct kinds of object, and indices cannot be moved around freely — which is exactly why we had to distinguish contravariance from covariance.

The Dirac notation from before now becomes transparent: “turning a ket into a bra” is precisely ♭\flat♭, except that the complex inner product is antilinear, so a rigorous statement carries a conjugation; conversely ∣v⟩=♯(⟨v∣)|v\rangle=\sharp(\langle v|)∣v⟩=♯(⟨v∣). In relativity we do exactly the same thing with ggg; the two languages describe one and the same structure.

With indices raised and lowered, the rule that only an upper–lower pair may be contracted can be relaxed: as long as the index structure closes, we can use the metric to align the indices first and then contract. For instance,

gμνVμVν=VμVμ=VνVνg_{\mu\nu}V^\mu V^\nu=V^\mu V_\mu=V_\nu V^\nugμν​VμVν=VμVμ​=Vν​Vν

is the “squared length” of VVV, a scalar independent of the choice of basis. In Minkowski spacetime, the norm of the four-momentum,
