6.1 The postulate of Classical Statistical Mechanics​

Time average and Ensemble average​

Unlike mechanical laws, which focus on predicting the dynamical behavior of systems based on specified initial conditions, the main purpose of thermodynamics lies in describing the equilibrium properties of macroscopic systems. In a sense, it is primarily concerned with stationary states.

From a measuring perspective, any measurement has time and spatial resolution. For example, the molecular density of room temperature air is approximately 102310^{23}1023 particles per liter, and the average molecular speed is about 400 m/s. If the spatial resolution of the measurement is 1μ1\mu1μm and the measurement duration is 1 ms, then the number of involved molecules in the experiment is approximately10810^{8}108, and each particle collides with another molecule10710^{7}107 times during the experiment process1. Therefore, a thermodynamic measurement can be viewed as an average over time TTT and volume VVV that are significantly larger than the microscopic characteristic scales. In fact, we can send TTT and VVV to infinity, after all, a thermodynamic measurement may extend, as we wish, to larger volumes and last for longer times than the example given above. The only thing to be careful is that, when taking the volume VVV to be approaching infinity, we also need to take the total number NNN of particles to be approaching infinity as well

V→∞,N→∞,VN=v(6.1)V \to \infty,\quad N \to \infty,\quad \frac{V}{N} = v \tag{6.1}V→∞,N→∞,NV​=v(6.1)

where the specific volume vvv remains unchanged. Eq. (6.1) defines the thermodynamic limit.

In classical mechanics, the physical quantity fff is expressed as a function of Hamiltonian variables qqq and p.p.p. Then, the main task of classical statistical mechanics is to evaluate the following time average2

fˉ=lim⁡T→∞1T∫0Tf[q(t′),p(t′)]dt′.(6.2)\bar{f} = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f[q(t'),p(t')]dt'. \tag{6.2}fˉ​=T→∞lim​T1​∫0T​f[q(t′),p(t′)]dt′.(6.2)

The Hamiltonian variables {q(t),p(t)}\{q(t), p(t)\}{q(t),p(t)} are determined by the Hamilton’s equation Eq. (0.1). As time evolves, it forms a trajectory in the phase space (spanned by 3N3N3N canonical coordinates and 3N3N3N canonical momenta). Since we are dealing with thermal equilibrium of NNN molecules without bulk motion, it is reasonable to assume that this trajectory repeatedly pass through a volume element d3Nqd3Npd^{3N}qd^{3N}pd3Nqd3Np located at (q,p)(q, p)(q,p) many times provided that TTT is sufficiently large. Let the time spent by the phase point inside this volume be denoted as τ,\tau,τ, then we can define a density function (unnormalized)

ρ(q,p) d3Nq d3Np=lim⁡T→∞τT.(6.3)\rho(q,p)\,d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\tau}{T}. \tag{6.3}ρ(q,p)d3Nqd3Np=T→∞lim​Tτ​.(6.3)

Next, we consider that the 6N6N6N-dimensional phase space is populated by a fluid with density ρ(q,p)\rho(q, p)ρ(q,p), where each point (q,p)(q, p)(q,p) corresponds to a fictitious copy of our system under study. All these imaginary copies is known as an ensemble. Then, the time average fˉ\bar{f}fˉ​ of a physical quantity fff can also be represented by the ensemble average

⟨f⟩=∫d3Nq d3Np ρ(q,p)f(q,p)∫d3Nq d3Np ρ(q,p).(6.4)\langle f\rangle = \frac{\int d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p\,\rho(q,p)f(q,p)}{\int d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p\,\rho(q,p)}. \tag{6.4}⟨f⟩=∫d3Nqd3Npρ(q,p)∫d3Nqd3Npρ(q,p)f(q,p)​.(6.4)

We now recall Liouville’s theorem, which states that the fictitious fluid in phase space is incompressible (dρ/dt=0),(d\rho/dt= 0),(dρ/dt=0),

dρdt=∂ρ∂t+∑i=13N(∂ρ∂qi∂H∂pi−∂ρ∂pi∂H∂qi)=0.(6.5)\frac{d\rho}{dt} = \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \sum_{i=1}^{3N}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial\mathscr{H}}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial\mathscr{H}}{\partial q_i}\right) = 0. \tag{6.5}dtdρ​=∂t∂ρ​+i=1∑3N​(∂qi​∂ρ​∂pi​∂H​−∂pi​∂ρ​∂qi​∂H​)=0.(6.5)

Since in equilibrium the density function ρ\rhoρ does not depend explicitly on time, Eq. (6.5) implies that the Poisson bracket {ρ,H}\{\rho,\mathscr{H}\}{ρ,H} vanishes. Consequently, ρ\rhoρ must be a function of the integrals of motion. In specific models there may exist many independent integrals of motion (for integrable systems, the number of such integrals is 3N).3N).3N). For realistic systems, however, the dynamics may be sufficiently complicated that only momentum, angular momentum, and energy can be identified as integrals of motion. Furthermore, by working in the center-of-mass frame and restricting attention to states with vanishing total momentum and angular momentum, we may effectively take the density function ρ(q,p)\rho(q, p)ρ(q,p) to depend only on energy:

ρ(q,p)=ϱ[H(q,p)].(6.6)\rho(q,p) = \varrho[\mathscr{H}(q,p)]. \tag{6.6}ρ(q,p)=ϱ[H(q,p)].(6.6)

It is emphasized that if the molecule number NNN is allowed to vary, the density function may also depend on it.

To further refine the form of ρ,\rho,ρ, other considerations are required.

Postulate of Equal a Priori Probability​

In an isolated system of 102310^{23}1023 molecules, its trajectory resides on a surface of constant energy EEE in phase space. Due to unavoidable perturbations (e.g., from measurement), this surface may be extended into a shell sandwiched between two surfaces of fixed energies EEE and E+Δ.E+\Delta.E+Δ. For sufficiently weak perturbations, we may assume

Δ≪E.(6.7)\Delta \ll E. \tag{6.7}Δ≪E.(6.7)

In addition, from quantum mechanical perspective, there is an energy-time uncertainty, thus Δ\DeltaΔ is unavoidable due to finite lifespan of experiments. Therefore, Δ\DeltaΔ should be considered as a real small but finite quantity, not a purely mathematical infinitesimal.

Given the intricate and chaotic molecular motion, it is attempting to assume that “every state” within the energy shell has been transiently visited for sufficiently long time TTT3. This principle, known as the ergodic hypothesis4, underlies the postulate of equal a priori probability. It is beyond the scope of this course to discuss the details of ergodic hypotheses. Instead, we will take it as the starting point to explore how it can reconcile experimental observations, after all, a correct physical theory can only be confirmed by experiments. The density function ρ\rhoρ is then assumed to have the following form

ρ(q,p)={const.,if E<H(q,p)<E+Δ,0,otherwise.(6.8)\rho(q,p) = \begin{cases} \text{const.}, & \text{if } E < \mathscr{H}(q,p) < E+\Delta, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \tag{6.8}ρ(q,p)={const.,0,​if E<H(q,p)<E+Δ,otherwise.​(6.8)

The corresponding ensemble is known as Microcanonical ensemble.

Fluctuations of Thermal quantities​

The ensemble average given in Eq. (6.4) is assumed to be some thermal quantities, which can be measured in experiments, connected to the phase function f(q,p).f(q, p).f(q,p). Unlike classical quantities, which are deterministic, thermodynamic quantities are random variables with mean square fluctuations

⟨(Δf)2⟩=⟨(f−⟨f⟩)2⟩=⟨f2⟩−⟨f⟩2.(6.9)\langle(\Delta f)^2\rangle = \langle(f-\langle f\rangle)^2\rangle = \langle f^2\rangle - \langle f\rangle^2. \tag{6.9}⟨(Δf)2⟩=⟨(f−⟨f⟩)2⟩=⟨f2⟩−⟨f⟩2.(6.9)

In thermodynamic systems comprising a vast number of particles, fluctuations are typically negligible compared to average values, thereby making statistical mechanics meaningful. In fact, we can prove

⟨(Δf)2⟩⟨f⟩≈1N.(6.10)\frac{\sqrt{\langle(\Delta f)^2\rangle}}{\langle f\rangle} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}. \tag{6.10}⟨f⟩⟨(Δf)2⟩​​≈N​1​.(6.10)

Essentially, the small relative fluctuation is due to the central limit theorem in probability theory. We shall present a simple argument to support this expression below.

Let us assume that the quantity fff is additive. When we decompose the entire system into NNN small parts, we find that

⟨f⟩=∑i=1N⟨fi⟩.(6.11)\langle f\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f_i\rangle. \tag{6.11}⟨f⟩=i=1∑N​⟨fi​⟩.(6.11)

Assuming these small portions, though individually small relative to the entire system, remain still large enough to be statistically independent, then for j≠k,j \ne k,j=k,

⟨ΔfjΔfk⟩=⟨Δfj⟩⟨Δfk⟩=0.(6.12)\langle\Delta f_j\Delta f_k\rangle = \langle\Delta f_j\rangle\langle\Delta f_k\rangle = 0. \tag{6.12}⟨Δfj​Δfk​⟩=⟨Δfj​⟩⟨Δfk​⟩=0.(6.12)

Therefore,

⟨(Δf)2⟩=∑i=1N⟨(Δfi)2⟩+∑j=1N∑k≠j,k=1N⟨(Δfj)(Δfk)⟩=∑i=1N⟨(Δfi)2⟩,(6.13)\langle(\Delta f)^2\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle(\Delta f_i)^2\rangle + \sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq j,k=1}^{N}\langle(\Delta f_j)(\Delta f_k)\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle(\Delta f_i)^2\rangle, \tag{6.13}⟨(Δf)2⟩=i=1∑N​⟨(Δfi​)2⟩+j=1∑N​k=j,k=1∑N​⟨(Δfj​)(Δfk​)⟩=i=1∑N​⟨(Δfi​)2⟩,(6.13)

which indicates that ⟨(Δf)2⟩\langle(\Delta f)^{2}\rangle⟨(Δf)2⟩ is proportional to N.N.N. Notice that ⟨f⟩\langle f\rangle⟨f⟩ is also proportional to N,N,N, thus we obtain

⟨(Δf)2⟩⟨f⟩∝1N.(6.14)\frac{\sqrt{\langle(\Delta f)^2\rangle}}{\langle f\rangle} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}. \tag{6.14}⟨f⟩⟨(Δf)2⟩​​∝N​1​.(6.14)

Since the fluctuation is negligibly small, we are allowed to replace the ensemble average by the most probable value of the physical quantities, which is possessed by the largest number of systems in the ensemble, or has the greatest probability.
