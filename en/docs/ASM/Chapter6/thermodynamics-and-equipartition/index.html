Derivation of Thermodynamics and Equipartition Theorem

6.3 Derivation of Thermodynamics​

We have defined the entropy SSS and temperature TTT of a system as functions of its energy E,E,E, volume V,V,V, and particle number N.N.N. In subsequent sections, other thermodynamic functions will be introduced. Together, these functions characterize the equilibrium state of the system. The detailed dynamical information about the microscopic motion of the constituent particles is encoded in these functions only in a statistical sense, and cannot be retrieved from them.

The values of these thermodynamic functions change as the system passes from one equilibrium state to another. Unless otherwise specified, each intermediate state during such a process is assumed to be in equilibrium. This can be realized by varying the system arbitrarily slowly; such a process is called quasistatic in thermodynamics. Throughout a quasistatic process, the thermodynamic functions remain well-defined at every stage. Of course, a real transition may deviate from equilibrium. In that case, the time evolution of the system must be taken into account, and the thermodynamic functions may need to be redefined or extended. We shall not consider non-equilibrium states for the time being.

Consider a quasistatic process involving infinitesimal changes in energy and volume, such that

dS(E,V)=(∂S∂E)VdE+(∂S∂V)EdV.(6.30)dS(E,V) = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_V dE + \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_E dV. \tag{6.30}dS(E,V)=(∂E∂S​)V​dE+(∂V∂S​)E​dV.(6.30)

We may define the pressure PPP as

P=T(∂S∂V)E,(6.31)P = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_E, \tag{6.31}P=T(∂V∂S​)E​,(6.31)

and, using the definition of temperature, we obtain

dS=1T(dE+PdV),ordE=TdS−PdV.(6.32)dS = \frac{1}{T}(dE + PdV),\quad \text{or}\quad dE = TdS - PdV. \tag{6.32}dS=T1​(dE+PdV),ordE=TdS−PdV.(6.32)

This is the fundamental thermodynamic relation, which expresses the first law of thermodynamics for reversible processes. Thus, we have shown how classical statistical mechanics can be used to derive the zeroth law (concerning temperature and thermal equilibrium), the first law (concerning energy conservation), and the second law (concerning the entropy of an isolated system).

  2. Calculate the entropy S(E,V)=kln⁡Σ(E)S(E, V) = k\ln\Sigma(E)S(E,V)=klnΣ(E).

  3. Solve EEE in terms of SSS and V.V.V. The resulting function is defined as the internal energy of the system and usually represented by U(S,V)U(S, V)U(S,V)(not E).E).E).

  4. Other thermodynamic functions can be calculated as follows
