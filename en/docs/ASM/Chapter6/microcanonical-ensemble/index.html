6.2 Microcanonical Ensemble

A microcanonical ensemble has a fixed molecule number NNN, a volume VVV, and an energy between EEE and E+Δ.E+ \Delta.E+Δ. We now define other thermodynamic quantities in this ensemble, of which the most important one is entropy.

Let Ω(E)\Omega(E)Ω(E) denote the volume in phase space enclosed by the surface with constant energy E,E,E,

Ω(E)≡1h3N∫H(q,p)<Ed3Nq d3Np.(6.15)\Omega(E) \equiv \frac{1}{h^{3N}}\int_{\mathscr{H}(q,p)<E} d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p. \tag{6.15}Ω(E)≡h3N1​∫H(q,p)<E​d3Nqd3Np.(6.15)

It depends also on VVV and N.N.N. Notice that Ω(E)\Omega(E)Ω(E) is given in unit of h3Nh^{3N}h3N with hhh the Planck’s constant, such that Ω(E)\Omega(E)Ω(E) is dimensionless. Thus, the phase volume occupied by the microcanonical ensemble is given by

Σ(E)=Ω(E+Δ)−Ω(E)=ω(E)Δ,(6.16)\Sigma(E) = \Omega(E+\Delta) - \Omega(E) = \omega(E)\Delta, \tag{6.16}Σ(E)=Ω(E+Δ)−Ω(E)=ω(E)Δ,(6.16) ω(E)≡∂Ω∂E=∫d3Nq d3Nph3N δ[E−H(q,p)],(6.17)\omega(E) \equiv \frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial E} = \int \frac{d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p}{h^{3N}}\,\delta[E-\mathscr{H}(q,p)], \tag{6.17}ω(E)≡∂E∂Ω​=∫h3Nd3Nqd3Np​δ[E−H(q,p)],(6.17)

where we introduce the density of state ω(E).\omega(E).ω(E). Following Boltzmann, the entropy is defined formally as

S(E,V)=kln⁡Σ(E),(6.18)S(E,V) = k\ln\Sigma(E), \tag{6.18}S(E,V)=klnΣ(E),(6.18)

where kkk is the Boltzmann constant. The quantities defined in Eqs. (6.15), (6.16), and (6.17) all grow exponentially with NNN, as will be demonstrated later for some specific systems such as ideal gases.

We shall demonstrate the following statements about entropy thus defined

  • Entropy is an extensive quantity.
  • In the isolated system, entropy never decreases.

Let us divide the isolated system at equilibrium into two subsystems by inserting a partition. The two subsystems contain N1N_{1}N1​ and N2N_{2}N2​ particles and occupy volumes V1V_{1}V1​ and V2,V_{2},V2​, respectively. The energy of subsystem 1 lies between E1E_{1}E1​ and E1+Δ,E_{1}+\Delta,E1​+Δ, while that of subsystem 2 lies between E2E_{2}E2​ and E2+Δ.E_{2}+ \Delta.E2​+Δ. The two subsystems with their respective energy intervals are shown in Fig. 6.1.

Figure 6.1: The microcanonical ensemble consisting of two subsystems.

The corresponding phase volumes of these two subsystems are denoted as Σ1(E1)\Sigma_{1}(E_{1})Σ1​(E1​) and Σ2(E2),\Sigma_{2}(E_{2}),Σ2​(E2​), then their entropies can be expressed as

S1(E1)=kln⁡Σ1(E1),S2(E2)=kln⁡Σ2(E2).(6.19)S_1(E_1) = k\ln\Sigma_1(E_1),\quad S_2(E_2) = k\ln\Sigma_2(E_2). \tag{6.19}S1​(E1​)=klnΣ1​(E1​),S2​(E2​)=klnΣ2​(E2​).(6.19)

For convenience, we omit the volumes V1V_{1}V1​ and V2V_{2}V2​ in these expressions. The total energy of the entire system lies between E=E1+E2E=E_{1}+E_{2}E=E1​+E2​ and E+2Δ.E+ 2\Delta.E+2Δ. Its phase volume can be written as

Σ(E)=∑E1/Δ=0E/ΔΣ1(E1)Σ2(E−E1),(6.20)\Sigma(E) = \sum_{E_1/\Delta=0}^{E/\Delta}\Sigma_1(E_1)\Sigma_2(E-E_1), \tag{6.20}Σ(E)=E1​/Δ=0∑E/Δ​Σ1​(E1​)Σ2​(E−E1​),(6.20)

where all energies are measured in units of Δ.\Delta.Δ. Among the summands there is a maximum term; let the corresponding subsystem energies be denoted Eˉ1\bar{E}_{1}Eˉ1​ and Eˉ2,\bar{E}_{2},Eˉ2​, with Eˉ2=E−Eˉ1.\bar{E}_{2}=E-\bar{E}_{1}.Eˉ2​=E−Eˉ1​. We then obtain the following inequalities for the phase volumes and entropies of the subsystems:

Σ1(Eˉ1)Σ2(Eˉ2)<Σ(E)<EΔΣ1(Eˉ1)Σ2(Eˉ2)⇒ln⁡[Σ1(Eˉ1)Σ2(Eˉ2)]<ln⁡[Σ(E)]<ln⁡[Σ1(Eˉ1)Σ2(Eˉ2)]+ln⁡(E/Δ).(6.21)\begin{aligned}\Sigma_1(\bar{E}_1)\Sigma_2(\bar{E}_2) < \Sigma(E) < \frac{E}{\Delta}\Sigma_1(\bar{E}_1)\Sigma_2(\bar{E}_2)\\ \Rightarrow \ln[\Sigma_1(\bar{E}_1)\Sigma_2(\bar{E}_2)] < \ln[\Sigma(E)] < \ln[\Sigma_1(\bar{E}_1)\Sigma_2(\bar{E}_2)] + \ln(E/\Delta). \end{aligned} \tag{6.21}Σ1​(Eˉ1​)Σ2​(Eˉ2​)<Σ(E)<ΔE​Σ1​(Eˉ1​)Σ2​(Eˉ2​)⇒ln[Σ1​(Eˉ1​)Σ2​(Eˉ2​)]<ln[Σ(E)]<ln[Σ1​(Eˉ1​)Σ2​(Eˉ2​)]+ln(E/Δ).​(6.21)

Note that Δ\DeltaΔ is a fixed small constant, and

ln⁡Σ1(Eˉ1)∝N1,ln⁡Σ2(Eˉ2)∝N2,E∝N1+N2,(6.22)\ln\Sigma_1(\bar{E}_1) \propto N_1,\quad \ln\Sigma_2(\bar{E}_2) \propto N_2,\quad E \propto N_1 + N_2, \tag{6.22}lnΣ1​(Eˉ1​)∝N1​,lnΣ2​(Eˉ2​)∝N2​,E∝N1​+N2​,(6.22)

therefore, we conclude that

S(E,V)=S1(Eˉ1,V1)+S2(Eˉ2,V2)+O(ln⁡(N))→N→∞S1(Eˉ1,V2)+S1(Eˉ2,V2).(6.23)\begin{aligned}S(E,V) &= S_1(\bar{E}_1,V_1) + S_2(\bar{E}_2,V_2) + O(\ln(N))\\ &\xrightarrow{N\to\infty} S_1(\bar{E}_1,V_2) + S_1(\bar{E}_2,V_2).\end{aligned} \tag{6.23}S(E,V)​=S1​(Eˉ1​,V1​)+S2​(Eˉ2​,V2​)+O(ln(N))N→∞​S1​(Eˉ1​,V2​)+S1​(Eˉ2​,V2​).​(6.23)

In other word, the entropy defined in Eq. (6.18) is an extensive quantity in the thermodynamic limit.

By an argument similar to that leading to inequality (6.21), we can also establish the inequality Σ(E)<Ω(E)<(E/Δ)Σ(E),\Sigma(E)\lt \Omega(E)\lt (E/\Delta) \Sigma(E),Σ(E)<Ω(E)<(E/Δ)Σ(E), which, in the thermodynamic limit, yields the identities

S(E,V)=kln⁡Σ(E)=kln⁡Ω(E)=kln⁡ω(E).(6.24)S(E,V) = k\ln\Sigma(E) = k\ln\Omega(E) = k\ln\omega(E). \tag{6.24}S(E,V)=klnΣ(E)=klnΩ(E)=klnω(E).(6.24)

We note that the dimension of ω(E)\omega(E)ω(E) is immaterial in this limit. Identities of this kind are typical in statistical mechanics.1

Because, under the equal a priori probability hypothesis, the probability of a given energy partition is proportional to the corresponding number of microstates Σ1(E1)Σ2(E2)\Sigma_{1}(E_{1})\Sigma_{2}(E_{2})Σ1​(E1​)Σ2​(E2​), inequality (6.21) implies that among all possible energy partitions, the most probable one—with subsystem energies Eˉ1\bar{E}_{1}Eˉ1​ and Eˉ2=E−Eˉ1\bar{E}_{2}=E-\bar{E}_{1}Eˉ2​=E−Eˉ1​—is overwhelmingly more likely than all other partitions. In other words, when the partition is inserted, the most probable energy partition is realized with probability effectively equal to 1, while all other partitions occur with probability effectively equal to 0. We may compute the entropy associated with a non-typical partition, say one with E1≠Eˉ1E_{1} \ne \bar{E}_{1}E1​=Eˉ1​ and E2=E−E1,E_{2}=E-E_{1},E2​=E−E1​, as follows:

Σ1(E1)Σ2(E−E1)<Σ1(Eˉ1)Σ2(Eˉ2)\Sigma_1(E_1)\Sigma_2(E-E_1) < \Sigma_1(\bar{E}_1)\Sigma_2(\bar{E}_2)Σ1​(E1​)Σ2​(E−E1​)<Σ1​(Eˉ1​)Σ2​(Eˉ2​) ⇒S1(E1,V1)+S2(E2,V2)<S1(Eˉ1,V1)+S2(Eˉ2,V2)=S(E,V).(6.25)\Rightarrow S_1(E_1,V_1) + S_2(E_2,V_2) < S_1(\bar{E}_1,V_1) + S_2(\bar{E}_2,V_2) = S(E,V). \tag{6.25}⇒S1​(E1​,V1​)+S2​(E2​,V2​)<S1​(Eˉ1​,V1​)+S2​(Eˉ2​,V2​)=S(E,V).(6.25)

The resulting entropy is smaller than that of the most probable partition. Therefore, we discover that the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease (the probability of such a decrease is almost zero). This is the second law of thermodynamics.

Notice that at the most probable values of the subsystem energies, the product Σ1(E1)Σ2(E2)\Sigma_{1}(E_{1})\Sigma_{2}(E_{2})Σ1​(E1​)Σ2​(E2​) is maximized subject to the constraint E1+E2=E,E_{1}+E_{2}=E,E1​+E2​=E, so that

δ[Σ1(E1)Σ2(E2)]=0,δE1+δE2=0.(6.26)\delta[\Sigma_1(E_1)\Sigma_2(E_2)] = 0,\quad \delta E_1 + \delta E_2 = 0. \tag{6.26}δ[Σ1​(E1​)Σ2​(E2​)]=0,δE1​+δE2​=0.(6.26)

This gives rise to the condition

[∂∂E1ln⁡Σ1(E1)]E1=Eˉ1=[∂∂E2ln⁡Σ2(E2)]E2=Eˉ2,(6.27)\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial E_1}\ln\Sigma_1(E_1)\right]_{E_1=\bar{E}_1} = \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial E_2}\ln\Sigma_2(E_2)\right]_{E_2=\bar{E}_2}, \tag{6.27}[∂E1​∂​lnΣ1​(E1​)]E1​=Eˉ1​​=[∂E2​∂​lnΣ2​(E2​)]E2​=Eˉ2​​,(6.27)

or equivalently,

[∂∂E1S1(E1,V1)]E1=Eˉ1=[∂∂E2S1(E2,V2)]E2=Eˉ2.(6.28)\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial E_1}S_1(E_1,V_1)\right]_{E_1=\bar{E}_1} = \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial E_2}S_1(E_2,V_2)\right]_{E_2=\bar{E}_2}. \tag{6.28}[∂E1​∂​S1​(E1​,V1​)]E1​=Eˉ1​​=[∂E2​∂​S1​(E2​,V2​)]E2​=Eˉ2​​.(6.28)

Let us define the temperature of the system by

∂S(E,V)∂E=1T.(6.29)\frac{\partial S(E,V)}{\partial E} = \frac{1}{T}. \tag{6.29}∂E∂S(E,V)​=T1​.(6.29)

Then, Eq. (6.28) is therefore equivalent to the statement that the two subsystems have the same temperature at equilibrium, a result known as the zeroth law of thermodynamics. If their energies deviate from the most probable values, the two temperatures will in general differ, and the subsystems are not in thermal equilibrium. When energy exchange between them is allowed, heat flows from one subsystem to the other, driving the combined system toward thermal equilibrium and thereby maximizing the entropy.
