7.5 Chemical Potential

Thermodynamics.​

In the canonical ensemble, the Helmholtz free energy AAA is a function of NNN, VVV, and TTT, with NNN fixed. In the grand canonical ensemble, the particle number is allowed to vary, so the chemical potential μ\muμ enters through an additional term μ dN\mu\,dNμdN. Taking these dependencies into account, we obtain:

dA=−PdV−SdT+μdN.(7.47)dA = -PdV - SdT + \mu dN. \tag{7.47}dA=−PdV−SdT+μdN.(7.47)

By applying the Legendre transformation U=A+TSU = A + TSU=A+TS, we obtain the first law of thermodynamics in its general form:

dU=TdS−PdV+μdN.(7.48)dU = TdS - PdV + \mu dN. \tag{7.48}dU=TdS−PdV+μdN.(7.48)

The Gibbs free energy is defined by another Legendre transformation: G=A+PVG = A + PVG=A+PV. Its differential form is:

dG=VdP−SdT+μdN.(7.49)dG = VdP - SdT + \mu dN. \tag{7.49}dG=VdP−SdT+μdN.(7.49)

From Eqs. (7.47)-(7.49), we obtain the following expressions for the chemical potential μ\muμ under different conditions:

μ=(∂U∂N)V,S=(∂A∂N)V,T=(∂G∂N)P,T.(7.50)\mu = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\right)_{V,S} = \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial N}\right)_{V,T} = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N}\right)_{P,T}. \tag{7.50}μ=(∂N∂U​)V,S​=(∂N∂A​)V,T​=(∂N∂G​)P,T​.(7.50)

As an example, let us derive the chemical potential of an ideal gas, for which the canonical partition function for a system of NNN particles is given by:

QN(V,T)=1N!h3N∫d3Nq d3Np e−β∑i=13Npi2/(2m)=1N!(Vλ3)N,(7.51)Q_N(V,T) = \frac{1}{N!h^{3N}}\int d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p\,e^{-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{3N}p_i^2/(2m)} = \frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{V}{\lambda^3}\right)^N, \tag{7.51}QN​(V,T)=N!h3N1​∫d3Nqd3Npe−β∑i=13N​pi2​/(2m)=N!1​(λ3V​)N,(7.51) λ=2πℏ2mkT,(thermal wavelength).(7.52)\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mkT}},\quad (\text{thermal wavelength}). \tag{7.52}λ=mkT2πℏ2​​,(thermal wavelength).(7.52)

The grand partition function is then:

Q(z,V,T)=∑N=0∞zNQN(V,T)=∑N=0∞1N!(zVλ3)N=ezV/λ3.(7.53)\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} z^N Q_N(V,T) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{zV}{\lambda^3}\right)^N = e^{zV/\lambda^3}. \tag{7.53}Q(z,V,T)=N=0∑∞​zNQN​(V,T)=N=0∑∞​N!1​(λ3zV​)N=ezV/λ3.(7.53)

The average particle number can be derived from Eq. (7.36)

⟨N⟩=z∂∂zln⁡Q(z,V,T)=zVλ3.(7.54)\langle N\rangle = z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = \frac{zV}{\lambda^3}. \tag{7.54}⟨N⟩=z∂z∂​lnQ(z,V,T)=λ3zV​.(7.54)

Finally, we obtain the chemical potential of an ideal gas in the following form

μ=kTln⁡(λ3n),n=⟨N⟩/V.(7.55)\mu = kT\ln(\lambda^3 n),\quad n = \langle N\rangle/V. \tag{7.55}μ=kTln(λ3n),n=⟨N⟩/V.(7.55)

Furthermore, the well-known equation of state for an ideal gas can also be derived: PV/(kT)=ln⁡Q(z,V,T)=zV/λ3=⟨N⟩PV/(kT) = \ln\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = zV/\lambda^3 = \langle N\ranglePV/(kT)=lnQ(z,V,T)=zV/λ3=⟨N⟩.

Recall that when the isothermal compressibility of the gas is finite, the grand partition function Q(z,V,T)\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T)Q(z,V,T) can be well approximated by its maximum term W(Nˉ)W(\bar{N})W(Nˉ). We shall demonstrate this statement using the ideal gas model. Note that the condition for maximizing ln⁡W(N)\ln W(N)lnW(N) is given by:

δln⁡W(N)=0⇔ln⁡W(N+1)−ln⁡W(N)=ln⁡[zN+1QN+1(V,T)zNQN(V,T)]=0.\delta\ln W(N) = 0 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \ln W(N+1) - \ln W(N) = \ln\left[\frac{z^{N+1}Q_{N+1}(V,T)}{z^N Q_N(V,T)}\right] = 0.δlnW(N)=0⇔lnW(N+1)−lnW(N)=ln[zNQN​(V,T)zN+1QN+1​(V,T)​]=0.

Substituting Eq. (7.51) into this equation, we obtain

Nˉ+1=zVλ3.\bar{N} + 1 = \frac{zV}{\lambda^3}.Nˉ+1=λ3zV​.

Hence, Nˉ≈⟨N⟩\bar{N} \approx \langle N\rangleNˉ≈⟨N⟩ in the thermodynamic limit. Then,

ln⁡W(Nˉ)=ln⁡[1Nˉ!(zVλ3)Nˉ]=ln⁡[(Nˉ+1)NˉNˉ!]≈Nˉln⁡(Nˉ+1)−Nˉln⁡Nˉ+Nˉ=Nˉln⁡(1+1Nˉ)+Nˉ=1+Nˉ=zVλ3=ln⁡Q(z,V,T).\begin{aligned} \ln W(\bar{N}) &= \ln\left[\frac{1}{\bar{N}!}\left(\frac{zV}{\lambda^3}\right)^{\bar{N}}\right] = \ln\left[\frac{(\bar{N}+1)^{\bar{N}}}{\bar{N}!}\right]\\ &\approx \bar{N}\ln(\bar{N}+1) - \bar{N}\ln\bar{N} + \bar{N} = \bar{N}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\bar{N}}\right) + \bar{N}\\ &= 1 + \bar{N} = \frac{zV}{\lambda^3} = \ln\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T). \end{aligned}lnW(Nˉ)​=ln[Nˉ!1​(λ3zV​)Nˉ]=ln[Nˉ!(Nˉ+1)Nˉ​]≈Nˉln(Nˉ+1)−NˉlnNˉ+Nˉ=Nˉln(1+Nˉ1​)+Nˉ=1+Nˉ=λ3zV​=lnQ(z,V,T).​

This equation justifies the conclusion that the grand partition function can be approximated by the most probable value of W(N)W(N)W(N)

Q(z,V,T)=∑N=0∞W(N)≈W(Nˉ).\mathcal{Q}(z,V,T) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} W(N) \approx W(\bar{N}).Q(z,V,T)=N=0∑∞​W(N)≈W(Nˉ).

Conservation of Particle Number​

In this part, we explore an interesting application of statistical physics to particle physics.

As established earlier, in the thermodynamic limit the grand canonical ensemble becomes equivalent to the canonical ensemble, since relative particle-number fluctuations vanish for large systems. In practice, the grand canonical ensemble provides a convenient approximation to the canonical ensemble even when the particle number is strictly conserved; the chemical potential then acts as a Lagrange multiplier that enforces a fixed average particle number.

In particle physics, baryon number conservation is a fundamental law: the total number of baryons (e.g., protons, neutrons) minus antibaryons is conserved. For instance, a proton can be created or annihilated alongside an antiproton. Thus, the proton number itself is not conserved. However, at low temperatures, thermal energy is insufficient to produce baryon-antibaryon pairs, and antibaryons are absent. In this regime, the proton (and neutron) number is effectively conserved.

A similar situation arises for electrons. At low temperatures, electron-positron pair production is negligible, and positrons are essentially absent, so the electron number is effectively conserved. At high temperatures, where the thermal energy exceeds the electron rest energy mc2mc^2mc2, the conserved quantity is instead the difference between the electron and positron numbers.

We now apply the grand canonical ensemble to analyze equilibrium in systems that allow particle–antiparticle pair creation and annihilation. The effective Hamiltonian is:

H=H1+H2−μ(N1−N2),(7.56)\mathscr{H} = \mathscr{H}_1 + \mathscr{H}_2 - \mu(N_1 - N_2), \tag{7.56}H=H1​+H2​−μ(N1​−N2​),(7.56)

where subscripts “1” and “2” denote particles and antiparticles, respectively, and μ\muμ is introduced as a Lagrange multiplier so that N1N_1N1​ and N2N_2N2​ can be treated as unconstrained variables. The grand partition function is:

Q=∑N1=0∞∑N2=0∞eβμ(N1−N2)QN1QN2=∑N1=0∞∑N2=0∞e−β[A(N1)+A(N2)−μ(N1−N2)]≈e−β[A(Nˉ1)+A(Nˉ2)−μ(Nˉ1−Nˉ2)],(7.57)\begin{aligned} \mathcal{Q} &= \sum_{N_1=0}^{\infty}\sum_{N_2=0}^{\infty} e^{\beta\mu(N_1-N_2)} Q_{N_1} Q_{N_2}\\ &= \sum_{N_1=0}^{\infty}\sum_{N_2=0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta[A(N_1)+A(N_2)-\mu(N_1-N_2)]}\\ &\approx e^{-\beta[A(\bar{N}_1)+A(\bar{N}_2)-\mu(\bar{N}_1-\bar{N}_2)]}, \end{aligned} \tag{7.57}Q​=N1​=0∑∞​N2​=0∑∞​eβμ(N1​−N2​)QN1​​QN2​​=N1​=0∑∞​N2​=0∑∞​e−β[A(N1​)+A(N2​)−μ(N1​−N2​)]≈e−β[A(Nˉ1​)+A(Nˉ2​)−μ(Nˉ1​−Nˉ2​)],​(7.57)

where only the dominant term is retained. The values Nˉ1\bar{N}_1Nˉ1​ and Nˉ2\bar{N}_2Nˉ2​ are determined by minimizing the exponent:

∂A(N1)∂N1=μ,∂A(N2)∂N2=−μ.(7.58)\frac{\partial A(N_1)}{\partial N_1} = \mu,\quad \frac{\partial A(N_2)}{\partial N_2} = -\mu. \tag{7.58}∂N1​∂A(N1​)​=μ,∂N2​∂A(N2​)​=−μ.(7.58)

To evaluate the Helmholtz free energies A(N1)A(N_1)A(N1​) and A(N2)A(N_2)A(N2​), a relativistic treatment would be preferable. For simplicity, however, we adopt the non-relativistic limit, in which the energy, including the rest energy, is given by E=mc2+p22mE = mc^2 + \frac{p^2}{2m}E=mc2+2mp2​. Using Eq. (7.55) with the chemical potential shifted by the rest energy, we obtain:

kTln⁡(λ3n1)+mc2=μ,kTln⁡(λ3n2)+mc2=−μ,(7.59)kT\ln(\lambda^3 n_1) + mc^2 = \mu,\quad kT\ln(\lambda^3 n_2) + mc^2 = -\mu, \tag{7.59}kTln(λ3n1​)+mc2=μ,kTln(λ3n2​)+mc2=−μ,(7.59)

where n1n_1n1​ and n2n_2n2​ are the most probable densities of particles and antiparticles, respectively. Adding these equations yields:

λ6n1n2=e−2mc2/(kT).(7.60)\lambda^6 n_1 n_2 = e^{-2mc^2/(kT)}. \tag{7.60}λ6n1​n2​=e−2mc2/(kT).(7.60)

The electron rest energy corresponds to a temperature of about 6×1096\times10^96×109 K, whereas the proton rest energy corresponds to a temperature roughly 2000 times higher. Hence, at ordinary temperatures the right-hand side of Eq. (7.60) is negligible, implying that at least one of n1n_1n1​ and n2n_2n2​ must be vanishingly small. This explains why antiparticles can be safely neglected when kT≪mc2kT \ll mc^2kT≪mc2.

Chemical Equilibrium​

In this part, we show application of statistical physics in chemistry.

Consider a very familiar chemical reaction

2H2+O2=2H2O.2\mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2 = 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}.2H2​+O2​=2H2​O.

It can also be rewritten as

2H2+O2−2H2O=0.2\mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2 - 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} = 0.2H2​+O2​−2H2​O=0.

Accordingly, a general reaction can be expressed as

∑i=1KνiXi=0,(7.61)\sum_{i=1}^{K}\nu_i X_i = 0, \tag{7.61}i=1∑K​νi​Xi​=0,(7.61)

where νi>0\nu_i > 0νi​>0 for reactants and νi<0\nu_i < 0νi​<0 for products.

The conservation law for this reaction is

δN1ν1=δN2ν2⋯=δNKνK,(7.62)\frac{\delta N_1}{\nu_1} = \frac{\delta N_2}{\nu_2}\cdots = \frac{\delta N_K}{\nu_K}, \tag{7.62}ν1​δN1​​=ν2​δN2​​⋯=νK​δNK​​,(7.62)

where δNi\delta N_iδNi​ is the number variation of the particle of the iiith type. It then allows us to write

δNi=νiδN,i=1,⋯ ,K.(7.63)\delta N_i = \nu_i\delta N,\quad i = 1,\cdots,K. \tag{7.63}δNi​=νi​δN,i=1,⋯,K.(7.63)

When the reaction balance is reached at constant VVV and TTT, the total Helmholtz free energy should be minimized,

0=δA=∑i=1KδAi=∑i=1K∂Ai∂NiδNi=∑i=1KμiνiδN.(7.64)0 = \delta A = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\delta A_i = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial N_i}\delta N_i = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_i\nu_i\delta N. \tag{7.64}0=δA=i=1∑K​δAi​=i=1∑K​∂Ni​∂Ai​​δNi​=i=1∑K​μi​νi​δN.(7.64)

Since δN≠0\delta N \neq 0δN=0, the condition for reaction equilibrium is

∑i=1Kμiνi=0,(7.65)\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_i\nu_i = 0, \tag{7.65}i=1∑K​μi​νi​=0,(7.65)

where μi\mu_iμi​ is the chemical potential of the molecule KiK_iKi​. If the reaction proceeds at constant PPP and TTT, we may equivalently use the Gibbs free energy, and the same condition is obtained.
