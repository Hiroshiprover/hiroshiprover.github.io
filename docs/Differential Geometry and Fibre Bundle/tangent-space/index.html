Tangent Vectors on Manifolds

Motivation​

Before introducing tangent vectors on manifolds, let us consider a familiar example to aid understanding. Consider a smooth function f(x,y)f(x, y)f(x,y) defined on R2\R^2R2. At a point ppp, since fff is smooth, we can freely take derivatives. For example, the gradient:

∇f=(∂f∂x,∂f∂y).\nabla{f}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).∇f=(∂x∂f​,∂y∂f​).

Note that if we set z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y) in R3\R^3R3, we obtain a 2‑dimensional surface F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)−zF(x, y, z) = f(x, y) - zF(x,y,z)=f(x,y)−z. According to multivariable calculus, the 3‑dimensional gradient vector is the normal vector to the tangent plane of this surface:

∇F=(∂F∂x,∂F∂y,∂F∂z)=(∂f∂x,∂f∂y,−1).\nabla{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial z}\right) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, -1\right).∇F=(∂x∂F​,∂y∂F​,∂z∂F​)=(∂x∂f​,∂y∂f​,−1).

The surface is 2‑dimensional, hence its tangent plane is also 2‑dimensional; vectors in it have two degrees of freedom. From the normal vector we can determine the general form of tangent vectors:

(a,b,g(a,b))⋅(∂f∂x,∂f∂y,−1)=0⟹T=(a,b,a∂f∂x+b∂f∂y),\left(a, b, g(a,b)\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, -1\right) = 0 \Longrightarrow T = \left(a, b, a\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + b \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right),(a,b,g(a,b))⋅(∂x∂f​,∂y∂f​,−1)=0⟹T=(a,b,a∂x∂f​+b∂y∂f​),

from which we can obviously extract two basis vectors:

rx=(1,0,∂f∂x), ry=(0,1,∂f∂y).r_x = \left(1, 0, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right), \ r_y = \left(0, 1, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).rx​=(1,0,∂x∂f​), ry​=(0,1,∂y∂f​).

If you are not familiar with this conclusion, try proving it yourself; consider only the origin, compute the tangent curves in the xxx‑ and yyy‑directions; these two curves determine the tangent plane, and you can write down the tangent vectors in the plane. The result is the same as here.

Notice that this definition of the tangent plane does not actually depend on the choice of the function: for any smooth function, we can perform the above operation and the two basis vectors obtained have the same form. In other words, this suggests establishing a correspondence:

rx→∂∂x, ry→∂∂y.r_x \to \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \ r_y \to \frac{\partial}{\partial y}.rx​→∂x∂​, ry​→∂y∂​.
note

You might think this is completely unjustified, but it is in fact a very elegant shortcut: it is a very special isomorphism – an isomorphism between the geometric tangent plane and the space of (first‑order) directional derivatives (a very small subspace of a function space). Of course, for finite‑dimensional vector spaces, as long as the dimensions are the same, they are always isomorphic because they have the same number of basis vectors. However, the isomorphism here is actually independent of the choice of basis; the appearance of a basis here is only because we describe the surface using a parametrisation. It can be shown that for any parametrisation the difference is only a Jacobian matrix.

note

If you define the tangent plane using equivalence classes of curves through ppp, then you can completely eliminate the choice of basis and obtain a natural isomorphism. However, such an introduction requires some background in classical differential geometry and a certain familiarity with the language of abstract algebra, so we will not go into further detail here.

Another illuminating viewpoint: you can think of the tangent plane as the best linear approximation to the surface at that point. This perspective is quite helpful for understanding Lie group theory.

We shall see that for a general manifold it is difficult to find a geometric tangent plane, but given a smooth scalar field we can always find this space of derivatives, and thus naturally extend the concept to general manifolds.

note

When we only consider problems in R3\R^3R3, the tangent plane is not actually a vector (sub)space, but rather an element of a quotient space – a coset (which can be understood as a “translation” of a subspace). Therefore speaking of a “basis” of the tangent plane is actually imprecise. Formally, when we write two 3‑dimensional vectors as a basis for a 2‑dimensional plane, we are shifting the tangent plane back to the origin.

Of course, these issues arise because we have “embedded” the surface in a 3‑dimensional Euclidean space, imposing a global coordinate system. This also explains why the tangent vectors we computed earlier take such a special, simple form: we chose the simplest embedding z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y), which directly assigns the function value as the height over R2\R^2R2, and naturally obtain the simplest basis, namely (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (1,0)(1,0)(1,0).

On a manifold, coordinates are local and the atlas is always maximal, so it is legitimate to call a tangent plane a vector space.

note

There is another way to understand vectors: purely consider the “differential” of a function, and then take its functional to obtain a vector. That approach is much clearer, but the price is that it requires very rigorous mathematics – a lot of abstract algebra and more rigorous analysis. We do not have sufficient mathematical background to adopt that approach. Curious readers may refer to Chern’s Lectures on Differential Geometry.

Definition: Tangent Vector​

Inspired by the initial example, we now define a tangent vector at a point on a manifold:

Tangent Vector

Let v:FM→Rv: \mathscr{F}_M \to \Rv:FM​→R be a map defined at a point ppp on the manifold MMM satisfying:

  1. v(αf+βg)=αv(f)+βv(g),∀α,β∈Rv(\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha v(f) + \beta v(g), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in \Rv(αf+βg)=αv(f)+βv(g),∀α,β∈R (linearity, homogeneity)
  2. v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+v(f)g(p)v(fg) = f(p)v(g) + v(f) g(p)v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+v(f)g(p) (Leibniz rule)

Then vvv is called a tangent vector at ppp. The first property is naturally required for a vector, and the second property is required for a derivative. A map satisfying the second property is also called a derivation.

note

We emphasise here that the map is “defined at the point ppp”: a vector on a manifold is a local concept. Without further structure, we cannot compare vectors at two different points.

In Rn\R^nRn we can perform such a comparison because Rn\R^nRn has a natural translation that relates vectors at different points – Rn\R^nRn is flat.

note

Some differential geometry textbooks, especially Liang Canbin’s Differential Geometry and General Relativity, like to directly call tangent vectors “vectors”. This is not very precise because in the context of fibre bundles, there are many other vectors on a manifold besides tangent vectors, for example vector bundles of Hilbert spaces over a parameter manifold. We will discuss this in detail later.

Definition: Tangent Space​

The set of all tangent vectors at a point ppp on a manifold MMM naturally forms a vector space, called the tangent space and denoted TpMT_pMTp​M. According to our geometric intuition, the dimension of this tangent space should equal the dimension of the manifold. To verify this conjecture, we need to find a basis. And as suggested by the initial example, a natural choice is to consider partial derivatives. Therefore we take the basis vectors as:

Xμ:FM→R,Xμ(f)=∂F∂xμ∣p,μ=1,2,…,n,X_\mu: \mathscr{F}_M \to \R, \quad X_\mu(f) = \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial x^\mu}\right|_{p}, \quad \mu = 1, 2, \dots, n,Xμ​:FM​→R,Xμ​(f)=∂xμ∂F​​p​,μ=1,2,…,n,

often abbreviated as ∂μ(f)=∂f∂xμ∣p\partial_\mu(f)=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\mu}\right|_{p}∂μ​(f)=∂xμ∂f​​p​. We call this basis the coordinate basis.

We have merely declared that {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​} can serve as a basis, but we have not proved this. Specifically, we need to prove:

  1. Linear independence of {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​}: i.e., if there exist {αμ}\{\alpha^\mu\}{αμ} such that αμ∂μ=0\alpha^\mu \partial_\mu = 0αμ∂μ​=0, then αμ=0\alpha^\mu = 0αμ=0 for all μ\muμ;

  2. Completeness of {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​}: i.e., for any vector vvv, it can be expanded as a linear combination of {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​}: v=vμ∂μv = v^\mu \partial_\muv=vμ∂μ​, where vμ=v(xμ)v^\mu = v(x^\mu)vμ=v(xμ) are the components of vvv in the coordinate basis.

As before, we omit the proofs. The proof of linear independence is obvious: use the coordinate functions xμx^\muxμ themselves. The proof of completeness is long and not straightforward; we only sketch the idea: expand the nnn-variable function corresponding to a scalar field in a Taylor series up to first order, keeping an integral remainder (or some other form of remainder), and then show that any derivation (tangent vector) acting on the zeroth‑order term and the remainder yields zero.
