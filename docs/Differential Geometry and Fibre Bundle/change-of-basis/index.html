Change of Basis

Finally, we now have enough basic machinery to answer a very fundamental question: why are the components of a vector written with upper indices, while those of a covector use lower indices, and why is it the opposite for basis vectors? This entire “tragedy” ultimately comes from physicists naming things according to their behavior under basis transformations.

Transformations of Four Basic Objects​

Transformation of Basis Vectors​

Consider a linear map

Λ:V→V,{eμ}↦{eμ′},\Lambda: V \to V, \quad \{e_\mu \} \mapsto \{e'_\mu\},Λ:V→V,{eμ​}↦{eμ′​},

This is clearly a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor (a matrix), and we have

eμ′=Λνμeν.e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu.eμ′​=Λνμ​eν​.

Here both eμe_\mueμ​ and eμ′e'_\mueμ′​ are actual vectors. Note that Λνμ\Lambda^\nu{}_\muΛνμ​ are the components of Λ\LambdaΛ with respect to the basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​}.

note

The symbol Λ\LambdaΛ is often used to denote a special class of transformations: Lorentz transformations, which arise from the requirement that the spacetime interval ds2\mathrm{d}s^2ds2 is invariant under coordinate changes. In our discussion, however, no such restriction is imposed.

Transformation of Vector Components​

Next we analyze how the components of a vector transform under the basis change Λ\LambdaΛ. Consider a vector vvv written in two different bases:

v=vμeμ=v′μeμ′.v = v^\mu e_\mu = v'^\mu e'_\mu.v=vμeμ​=v′μeμ′​.

Substituting the basis transformation gives

v′μΛνμeν=vνeν,v'^\mu \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu = v^\nu e_\nu,v′μΛνμ​eν​=vνeν​,

hence

v′μ=(Λ−1)μνvν.v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.v′μ=(Λ−1)μν​vν.
note

In tensor language, the inverse of a matrix is a tensor satisfying

Λμρ(Λ−1)ρν=δμν.\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho (\Lambda^{-1})^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu.Λμρ​(Λ−1)ρν​=δμν​.

Transformation of the Dual Basis​

Starting from the definition of the dual basis,

δμν=eμ(eν),\delta^\mu{}_\nu = e^\mu(e_\nu),δμν​=eμ(eν​),

we multiply both sides by Λνρe′ρ\Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e'^\rhoΛνρ​e′ρ, obtaining

Λμρe′ρ=eμ(e′ρΛνρeν)=eμ(e′ρeρ′)=eμ.\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho e'^\rho = e^\mu(e'^\rho \Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e_\nu) = e^\mu(e'^\rho e'_\rho) = e^\mu.Λμρ​e′ρ=eμ(e′ρΛνρ​eν​)=eμ(e′ρeρ′​)=eμ.

Multiplying by (Λ−1)σμ(\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu(Λ−1)σμ​ yields

e′σ=(Λ−1)σμeμ.e'^\sigma = (\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu e^\mu.e′σ=(Λ−1)σμ​eμ.

Renaming dummy indices gives

e′μ=(Λ−1)μνeν.e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu.e′μ=(Λ−1)μν​eν.

Transformation of Covector Components​

Similarly, starting from

ω=ωμeμ=ωμ′e′μ,\omega = \omega_\mu e^\mu = \omega'_\mu e'^\mu,ω=ωμ​eμ=ωμ′​e′μ,

and substituting the basis transformation, we obtain

ωμ′=Λνμων.\omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu.ωμ′​=Λνμ​ων​.

Contravariance and Covariance​

We now observe a unified pattern:

Contravariance and Covariance

The transformations of basis vectors and covector components are

eμ′=Λνμeν,ωμ′=Λνμων,e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu, \qquad \omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu,eμ′​=Λνμ​eν​,ωμ′​=Λνμ​ων​,

while the transformations of dual basis vectors and vector components are

e′μ=(Λ−1)μνeν,v′μ=(Λ−1)μνvν.e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu, \qquad v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.e′μ=(Λ−1)μν​eν,v′μ=(Λ−1)μν​vν.

We see that any object carrying an upper index transforms with the inverse of the basis transformation, while any object with a lower index transforms with the transformation itself.

Thus, upper indices are called contravariant indices, and lower indices are called covariant indices; vectors are contravariant, and covectors are covariant (which is why dual vectors are also called covectors).

This explains why Einstein’s summation convention is defined in this way: indices do not merely indicate tensor type, but rather encode transformation behavior. Hence vectors use upper indices and covectors use lower indices, while repeated upper–lower pairs indicate summation, i.e. contraction, describing how tensors interact.

info

For a general tensor, the coordinate transformation law is

Tμ1⋯μkν1⋯νl=(Λ−1)μ1ρ1⋯(Λ−1)μkρkΛσ1ν1⋯ΛσlνlTρ1⋯ρkσ1⋯σl.T^{\mu_1\cdots \mu_k}{}_{\nu_1\cdots \nu_l} = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mu_1}{}_{\rho_1}\cdots(\Lambda^{-1})^{\mu_k}{}_{\rho_k} \Lambda^{\sigma_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots\Lambda^{\sigma_l}{}_{\nu_l} T^{\rho_1\cdots \rho_k}{}_{\sigma_1\cdots \sigma_l}.Tμ1​⋯μk​ν1​⋯νl​​=(Λ−1)μ1​ρ1​​⋯(Λ−1)μk​ρk​​Λσ1​ν1​​⋯Λσl​νl​​Tρ1​⋯ρk​σ1​⋯σl​​.

We omit the proof.

note

Many physics textbooks actually take this transformation law as the definition of a tensor: a (k,l)(k,l)(k,l)-tensor is defined as a collection of components satisfying the above rule, without explicit reference to bases. This definition is correct, but somewhat lacks conceptual depth.
