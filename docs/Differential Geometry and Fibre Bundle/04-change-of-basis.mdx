Finally, we now have enough basic machinery to answer a very fundamental question: why are the components of a vector written with upper indices, while those of a covector use lower indices, and why is it the opposite for basis vectors? This entire “tragedy” ultimately comes from physicists naming things according to their behavior under **basis transformations**.

## Transformations of Four Basic Objects {#transformations-of-four-basic-objects}

### Transformation of Basis Vectors {#transformation-of-basis-vectors}

Consider a linear map

$$
\Lambda: V \to V, \quad \{e_\mu \} \mapsto \{e'_\mu\},
$$

This is clearly a $(1,1)$-tensor (a matrix), and we have

$$
e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu.
$$

Here both $e_\mu$ and $e'_\mu$ are actual vectors. Note that $\Lambda^\nu{}_\mu$ are the components of $\Lambda$ with respect to the basis $\{e_\mu\}$.

:::note
The symbol $\Lambda$ is often used to denote a special class of transformations: Lorentz transformations, which arise from the requirement that the spacetime interval $\mathrm{d}s^2$ is invariant under coordinate changes. In our discussion, however, no such restriction is imposed.
:::

### Transformation of Vector Components {#transformation-of-vector-components}

Next we analyze how the components of a vector transform under the basis change $\Lambda$. Consider a vector $v$ written in two different bases:

$$
v = v^\mu e_\mu = v'^\mu e'_\mu.
$$

Substituting the basis transformation gives

$$
v'^\mu \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu = v^\nu e_\nu,
$$

hence

$$
v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.
$$

:::note
In tensor language, the inverse of a matrix is a tensor satisfying
$$
\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho (\Lambda^{-1})^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu.
$$
:::

### Transformation of the Dual Basis {#transformation-of-the-dual-basis}

Starting from the definition of the dual basis,

$$
\delta^\mu{}_\nu = e^\mu(e_\nu),
$$

we multiply both sides by $\Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e'^\rho$, obtaining

$$
\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho e'^\rho
= e^\mu(e'^\rho \Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e_\nu)
= e^\mu(e'^\rho e'_\rho)
= e^\mu.
$$

Multiplying by $(\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu$ yields

$$
e'^\sigma = (\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu e^\mu.
$$

Renaming dummy indices gives

$$
e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu.
$$

### Transformation of Covector Components {#transformation-of-covector-components}

Similarly, starting from

$$
\omega = \omega_\mu e^\mu = \omega'_\mu e'^\mu,
$$

and substituting the basis transformation, we obtain

$$
\omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu.
$$

## Contravariance and Covariance {#contravariance-and-covariance}

We now observe a unified pattern:

:::tip[Contravariance and Covariance]

The transformations of basis vectors and covector components are

$$
e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu, \qquad \omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu,
$$

while the transformations of dual basis vectors and vector components are

$$
e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu, \qquad v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.
$$

We see that any object carrying an upper index transforms with the **inverse** of the basis transformation, while any object with a lower index transforms with the transformation **itself**.

Thus, upper indices are called **contravariant indices**, and lower indices are called **covariant indices**; vectors are contravariant, and covectors are covariant (which is why dual vectors are also called covectors).
:::

This explains why Einstein’s summation convention is defined in this way: **indices do not merely indicate tensor type, but rather encode transformation behavior**. Hence vectors use upper indices and covectors use lower indices, while repeated upper–lower pairs indicate summation, i.e. contraction, describing how tensors interact.

:::info
For a general tensor, the coordinate transformation law is

$$
T^{\mu_1\cdots \mu_k}{}_{\nu_1\cdots \nu_l}
=
(\Lambda^{-1})^{\mu_1}{}_{\rho_1}\cdots(\Lambda^{-1})^{\mu_k}{}_{\rho_k}
\Lambda^{\sigma_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots\Lambda^{\sigma_l}{}_{\nu_l}
T^{\rho_1\cdots \rho_k}{}_{\sigma_1\cdots \sigma_l}.
$$

We omit the proof.
:::

:::note
Many physics textbooks actually take this transformation law as the definition of a tensor: a $(k,l)$-tensor is defined as a collection of components satisfying the above rule, without explicit reference to bases. This definition is correct, but somewhat lacks conceptual depth.
:::
