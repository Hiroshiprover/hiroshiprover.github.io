Differential Geometry and Fibre Bundle for Babies

Preface

It is well known that textbooks, notes, and lecture materials in mathematics and physics have a long-standing tradition of deliberately presenting content in an obscure and difficult-to-understand manner. This practice—erasing the traces of thought processes while leaving only elegant results—has created a deep and formidable barrier between beginners and mathematical knowledge. This became especially evident to me when I attempted to self-study differential geometry, general relativity, fiber bundles, and gauge theory.

With this mindset, I began writing these notes, attempting to rephrase the abstract and concise formal language of differential geometry and fiber bundles in a more fundamental and accessible way, aiming to reconstruct the clean traces of previous thinkers' insights and to offer some intuitive guidance for concepts that are often difficult to grasp.

During the learning process, I realized that I was gradually losing the ability to express certain ideas in plain language—perhaps this is a kind of intellectual contamination.

Chapter 1: Mathematical Foundations

1.1 Topology​

It's assumed that you have basic knowledge of sets, including the definition, Cartesian product, etc.

In fact, the majority of differential geometry does not require much of the knowledge of topology, at least not the basic definitions. It's suggested that beginners should skip this chapter and read the followings directly.

Topology​

When mathematicians and physicists refer to "topology", there are some subtle differences involved. Here we focus on the rigorous definition of topology:

Topology

A set T\mathscr{T}T is called the topology of a set XXX, if T\mathscr{T}T contains all of the "open subsets" of XXX.

To assure the definition is self-consistent (all of the open subsets are contained), T\mathscr{T}T should satisfy the following conditions:

  1. X,∅∈TX, \varnothing \in \mathscr{T}X,∅∈T;
  2. if  Oi∈T,i=1,2,…,n\ O_i \in \mathscr{T}, i = 1, 2, \dots, n Oi​∈T,i=1,2,…,n, then ⋂i=1nOi∈T\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^n O_i \in \mathscr{T}i=1⋂n​Oi​∈T;
  3. if Oi∈T,∀i\ O_i \in \mathscr{T}, \forall i Oi​∈T,∀i, then ⋃iOi∈T\bigcup\limits_{i} O_i \in \mathscr{T}i⋃​Oi​∈T.

Notice that condition 2 require nnn to be a finite integer, while condition 3 does not. This means only finite intersections remains open, while the union of open sets remains open, even if we take infinite sets. For example, consider: Un={x∣∣x∣<1+1n}U_n = \{x | \left|x\right| < 1+ \frac{1}{n} \}Un​={x∣∣x∣<1+n1​}, then ⋂i=1∞Un→[−1,1]\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^\infty U_n \to [-1, 1]i=1⋂∞​Un​→[−1,1], not a open set of R\mathbb{R}R!

For an arbitrary set XXX, we define T\mathscr{T}T to be the set containing all subsets of XXX. This is a boring topology.

Continuity​

For a function f(x)f(x)f(x) in the usual sense (defined on R\mathbb{R}R), we define its continuity using the ε−δ\varepsilon - \deltaε−δ language:

If ∀ε>0\forall \varepsilon > 0∀ε>0, ∃δ>0\exists \delta > 0∃δ>0, such that whenever ∣f(x)−f(x0)∣<ε\left|f(x) - f(x_0)\right| < \varepsilon∣f(x)−f(x0​)∣<ε, ∣x−x0∣<δ\left|x - x_0\right| < \delta∣x−x0​∣<δ follows.

However, currently we only have a topological space, on which there is likely no well-defined addition, nor can we define an "order", thus making it impossible to compare magnitudes. At the same time, we do not have a "metric" to measure the "distance" between two elements. Therefore, we turn to seek ways to expand the definition of continuity. For topological spaces, we only have the means to measure open sets, so we need to rephrase the ε−δ\varepsilon - \deltaε−δ language in the language of sets:

If for any open subset UUU of the image f[X]f[X]f[X] of the mapping fff, its preimage f−1[U]f^{-1}[U]f−1[U] is an open subset, then fff is continuous.

At first glance, this definition seems to make no sense at all, but we can indeed prove that it is equivalent to the ε−δ\varepsilon - \deltaε−δ language on R\mathbb{R}R (in fact, it holds for any topological space equipped with a metric). We will skip the proof of this equivalence, as it is not necessary for physicists to pay too much attention on the details. However, the proof is quite simple (try using the definition of an open ball).

For a general function, we use CrC^rCr to denote that all derivatives up to the rrr-th order exist and are continuous. In particular, C0C^0C0 refers to continuous functions and C∞C^\inftyC∞ to smooth functions. We will adopt this notation for more general mappings as well. Homeomorphism​

A homeomorphism is a mapping used to measure how similar two topological spaces are. Specifically, we define:

Homeomorphism

If there exists a mapping fff between two topological spaces (X,T)(X, \mathscr{T})(X,T) and (Y,S)(Y, \mathscr{S})(Y,S) that satisfies the following conditions:

  1. fff is a bijection;
  2. Both fff and f−1f^{-1}f−1 are continuous mappings. Then these two topological spaces are said to be homeomorphic to each other, and fff is a homeomorphism mapping.
Note

Generally speaking, if fff is a continuous mapping and its inverse exists, then its inverse mapping is also continuous. However, you can force a counterexample by using the discrete topology and the trivial topology mentioned earlier:

Let the topology of (X,T)(X, \mathscr{T})(X,T) be the discrete topology (where all subsets are open subsets), and the topology of (Y,S)(Y, \mathscr{S})(Y,S) be the indiscrete topology (where only YYY and ∅\varnothing∅ are open subsets). Then, for any bijection fff, it is continuous: only Y=f[X]Y = f[X]Y=f[X] is an open subset, and its preimage f−1[Y]=Xf^{-1}[Y] = Xf−1[Y]=X is also an open set; however, for any inverse mapping f−1f^{-1}f−1, it is not continuous: any subset U⊆XU \subseteq XU⊆X is an open subset, but its preimage f−1[U]f^{-1}[U]f−1[U] is not an open subset, unless U=XU = XU=X.

This is almost the only application of discrete topology and coherent topology, and it is as boring as their definitions.

1.2 Linear Algebra​

We assume the reader already has some basic familiarity with linear algebra, such as the definitions of vector spaces and bases.

There are several concepts that may not appear in an introductory linear algebra course. For those, I will state the basic definitions here. For more detailed discussions, one may consult Linear Algebra Done Right or any standard advanced linear algebra textbook.

Readers may notice that some notation conventions differ from those used in other areas. In most cases they refer to exactly the same mathematical objects, so there is no reason to panic.

Vector Spaces​

In linear algebra, every finite-dimensional vector space admits a basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​}, such that any vector can be expanded with respect to this basis.

Index conventions

Readers may be more familiar with using i,j,ki,j,ki,j,k as indices, or explicitly writing 1,2,3,…1,2,3,\dots1,2,3,….

In differential geometry, Greek letters μ,ν,ρ,…\mu,\nu,\rho,\dotsμ,ν,ρ,… are typically used for vectors and their components. This is purely a convention.

In the context of general relativity (four-dimensional spacetime), one usually assumes:

μ,ν,ρ,⋯=0,1,2,3,\mu,\nu,\rho,\dots = 0,1,2,3,μ,ν,ρ,⋯=0,1,2,3,

where 000 denotes the time component.

Meanwhile, due to historical convention, one often writes

i,j,k,⋯=1,2,3i,j,k,\dots = 1,2,3i,j,k,⋯=1,2,3

for spatial indices.

Ordinary Roman letters such as a,b,ca,b,ca,b,c are often used as abstract indices. They do not take numerical values, but only indicate tensor type. This is a somewhat subtle topic which we will discuss later.

Once a basis is chosen, any vector can be expanded as

v=∑μvμeμ.v=\sum_\mu v^\mu e_\mu.v=μ∑​vμeμ​.

Notice that vector components are written with upper indices here. This is a common convention in differential geometry, and later we will see that it is actually quite natural.

Einstein Summation Convention​

So far everything is just standard linear algebra.

Now we begin preparing for differential geometry. In differential geometry, expressions with four or more summation signs frequently appear (for example, the full contraction of the Riemann curvature tensor into the Ricci scalar). Writing out every summation sign quickly becomes visually exhausting.

To avoid this, we introduce the Einstein summation convention.

Einstein summation convention

Whenever an upper and lower index with the same symbol appears repeatedly, summation over that index is implied.

For example,

vμeμ=∑μvμeμ,v^\mu e_\mu = \sum_\mu v^\mu e_\mu,vμeμ​=μ∑​vμeμ​,

or

Aμνvν≡∑νAμνvν.A^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu \equiv \sum_\nu A^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.Aμν​vν≡ν∑​Aμν​vν.

At the moment, we do not need to worry too much about what all these symbols mean. Their meaning will become clearer later.

For now, one may simply view this as a symbolic rule.

note

Physicists often suppress the basis entirely and define vectors directly through their components vμv^\muvμ, together with transformation laws.

This approach is often more intuitive, but somewhat less structural.

Here we adopt a more mathematical viewpoint, which is useful when discussing invariants and canonical objects.

In particular, we emphasize:

  1. vμv^\muvμ are the components of the vector vvv in the basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​};
  2. {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} are actual vectors.

Dual Vector Spaces​

Dual vectors​

We first define dual vectors.

The Dual of the Dual: Natural Isomorphism​

Since V∗V^*V∗ is itself a vector space, we can define its dual space V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗.

This process can clearly be repeated indefinitely, which naturally raises suspicion: do we really need to keep dualizing forever?

Fortunately, there is a canonical relation between VVV and V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗.

For any v∈Vv\in Vv∈V, define a linear functional on V∗V^*V∗ by

In other words,

V≅V∗∗.V\cong V^{**}.V≅V∗∗.

This allows vectors and dual vectors to pair naturally:

Similarly,

Hence,

ωμvμ=vμωμ.\omega_\mu v^\mu=v^\mu\omega_\mu.ωμ​vμ=vμωμ​.

This may look trivial, but it illustrates an important principle:

as long as index structure is correct, components may be manipulated like ordinary scalars, without constantly worrying about compositions of maps.

This is one reason why Einstein summation notation is so powerful.

note

If the equations above seem mysterious, write the bases explicitly and examine the interaction between basis vectors and dual basis vectors.

Remember that components are ordinary real numbers, while dual vectors are linear maps.

warning

All discussion above assumes finite-dimensional vector spaces.

For infinite-dimensional spaces, V∗V^*V∗ is typically larger than VVV, and V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗ is usually larger than VVV as well.

This is one reason why quantum mechanics, despite involving vectors (kets), dual vectors (bras), and tensors (operators), usually does not directly use Einstein summation convention.

In particular, one must be careful with states such as ∣x⟩|x\rangle∣x⟩ and ∣p⟩|p\rangle∣p⟩.

Interested readers may look into rigged Hilbert spaces.

1.3 Tensors​

In matrix language, vectors are represented as column vectors, while covectors (another name for dual vectors) are represented as row vectors. Then what exactly is a matrix? We will use this question as motivation to introduce the definition of tensors.

Motivation​

Let us first consider the so-called quadratic form:

xTAx=[x1x2⋯xn][a11a12⋯a1na21a22⋯a2n⋮⋮⋱⋮an1an2⋯ann][x1x2⋮xn].x^TAx=\begin{bmatrix} x_1&x_2&\cdots&x_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a^1{}_1&a^1{}_2&\cdots&a^1{}_n \\ a^2{}_1&a^2{}_2&\cdots&a^2{}_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a^n{}_1&a^n{}_2&\cdots&a^n{}_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x^1\\x^2\\ \vdots \\ x^n \end{bmatrix} .xTAx=[x1​​x2​​⋯​xn​​]​a11​a21​⋮an1​​a12​a22​⋮an2​​⋯⋯⋱⋯​a1n​a2n​⋮ann​​​​x1x2⋮xn​​.

Notice that the object on the left is a covector (row vector), while the object on the right is a vector (column vector), and the entire quadratic form evaluates to a real number.

Therefore, we may think of a matrix as a map sending a covector and a vector to a real number.

note

Of course, we also know that matrices can represent linear maps between vector spaces, or changes of basis. We will return to this perspective later.

More generally, let us allow different vectors on the two sides of the quadratic form. We then observe that matrices are linear in both the covector and vector arguments:

(αxT+βyT)Az=αxTAz+βyTAz,xTA(αy+βz)=αxTAy+βxTAz.(\alpha x^T + \beta y^T)Az = \alpha x^TAz + \beta y^TAz, \qquad x^TA(\alpha y + \beta z) = \alpha x^TAy + \beta x^TAz.(αxT+βyT)Az=αxTAz+βyTAz,xTA(αy+βz)=αxTAy+βxTAz.

Although this observation may seem elementary, it suggests a more general viewpoint: rather than viewing a matrix merely as a map from one vector space to another, we may regard it as a bilinear map defined on a vector space VVV and its dual space V∗V^*V∗.

This naturally leads us to more general constructions.

Tensor​

Let us first define bilinear maps rigorously, or more generally, multilinear maps.

Cartesian Product

The product symbol between vector spaces denotes the Cartesian product. The Cartesian product simply collects elements from two spaces into ordered pairs:

V×W:={(v,w)∣v∈V, w∈W}.V\times W:=\{(v,w)\mid v\in V,\ w\in W\}.V×W:={(v,w)∣v∈V, w∈W}.

In particular, for repeated Cartesian products of the same vector space, we use exponential notation:

Vn:=V×V×⋯×V⏟n times.V^n:=\underbrace{V\times V\times\cdots\times V}_{n\text{ times}}.Vn:=n timesV×V×⋯×V​​.

Cartesian products satisfy various basic properties. For example, the Cartesian product of two vector spaces is again a vector space, and the Cartesian product of two subspaces is isomorphic to their direct sum. We will not discuss these facts in detail here.

More generally, Cartesian products can be defined for arbitrary sets, not only vector spaces.

We can now define tensors in a simple way:

A tensor of type (k,l)(k,l)(k,l) is a multilinear map defined on V∗k×Vl{V^*}^k\times V^lV∗k×Vl. Equivalently, a (k,l)(k,l)(k,l)-tensor is a multilinear map that takes kkk covectors and lll vectors as inputs, and outputs a real number.

note

You may wonder why the dual space is placed before the vector space. In fact, this is because we are considering the objects on which tensors act, i.e. the domain of the tensor. If we consider what a tensor actually is, we will find that the front becomes vectors and the back becomes covectors. Specifically, any tensor can be written as:

Here, {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​} is the basis of the vector space VVV, while {eν}\{e^\nu\}{eν} is the dual basis.

A covector maps a vector to a real number, while a vector may also be viewed as mapping a covector to a real number. Therefore, under our definition, covectors and vectors can be regarded as tensors of type (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) respectively. In particular, a scalar (real number) is called a (0,0)(0,0)(0,0)-tensor.

Tensor Operations​

Tensor Addition and Scalar Multiplication​

From the definition above, we can naturally define tensor addition and scalar multiplication:

  1. (T+S)(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl)=T(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl)+S(ω1,⋯ ,ωk;v1,⋯ ,vl),(T+S)(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l)=T(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l)+S(\omega^1,\cdots,\omega^k;v_1,\cdots,v_l),(T+S)(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​)=T(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​)+S(ω1,⋯,ωk;v1​,⋯,vl​),

Tensor Product​

We begin with some motivation from matrices.

Consider the following fact:

Contraction​

We again begin with matrices. A matrix acts on a vector and produces another vector. In tensor language, this means that a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor can combine with a (1,0)(1,0)(1,0)-tensor to produce a new (1,0)(1,0)(1,0)-tensor.

Write a matrix as A=Aμν eμ⊗eν,A=A^\mu{}_\nu\,e_\mu\otimes e^\nu,A=Aμν​eμ​⊗eν, which is a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor. Now, let v=vρeρv=v^\rho e_\rhov=vρeρ​ be a vector, in linear algebra, matrix multiplication produces a new vector:

Well, the most basic way to combine tensors is first to take their tensor product, therefore we consider:

A⊗v=Aμνvρ eμ⊗eν⊗eρ,A\otimes v = A^\mu{}_\nu v^\rho\, e_\mu\otimes e^\nu\otimes e_\rho,A⊗v=Aμν​vρeμ​⊗eν⊗eρ​,

Define

where eμe^\mueμ is inserted into the iii-th covector slot, eμe_\mueμ​ into the jjj-th vector slot, and we sum over μ\muμ. Equivalently, contraction uses the fact that eμ(eν)=δμνe^\mu(e_\nu)=\delta^\mu{}_\nueμ(eν​)=δμν​ to eliminate one upper-lower index pair.

C12(A⊗v)=C12(Aμνvρ eμ⊗eν⊗eρ).C^2_1(A\otimes v) = C^2_1\left( A^\mu{}_\nu v^\rho\, e_\mu\otimes e^\nu\otimes e_\rho \right).C12​(A⊗v)=C12​(Aμν​vρeμ​⊗eν⊗eρ​).

which is exactly the expected result of matrix multiplication.

Thus, matrix multiplication is, in tensor language, tensor product + contraction. This idea generalizes to arbitrary tensors. Although in general, there is no unique multiplication rule between tensors, we can always first take tensor products, and then specify which pairs of upper and lower indices should be contracted. Different contraction choices usually lead to different results.

For example, in the same setting, we do a different contraction:

tr(A)=Aμμ.\mathrm{tr}(A)=A^\mu{}_\mu.tr(A)=Aμμ​.

Thus, contraction can be understood as one of the most fundamental interaction rules between tensors: it lowers tensor rank by pairing an upper index with a lower index.

1.4 More about indices: basis transformation​

We now have enough basic machinery to answer a very fundamental question: why are the components of a vector written with upper indices, while those of a covector use lower indices, and why is it the opposite for basis vectors? This entire “tragedy” ultimately comes from physicists naming things according to their behavior under basis transformations.

Transformations​

Basis Vectors​

Consider a linear map

Λ:V→V,{eμ}↦{eμ′},\Lambda: V \to V, \quad \{e_\mu \} \mapsto \{e'_\mu\},Λ:V→V,{eμ​}↦{eμ′​},

This is clearly a (1,1)(1,1)(1,1)-tensor (a matrix), and we have

eμ′=Λνμeν.e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu.eμ′​=Λνμ​eν​.

Here both eμe_\mueμ​ and eμ′e'_\mueμ′​ are actual vectors. Note that Λνμ\Lambda^\nu{}_\muΛνμ​ are the components of Λ\LambdaΛ with respect to the basis {eμ}\{e_\mu\}{eμ​}.

note

The symbol Λ\LambdaΛ is often used to denote a special class of transformations: Lorentz transformations, which arise from the requirement that the spacetime interval ds2\mathrm{d}s^2ds2 is invariant under coordinate changes. In our discussion, however, no such restriction is imposed.

Vector Components​

Next we analyze how the components of a vector transform under the basis change Λ\LambdaΛ. Consider a vector vvv written in two different bases:

v=vμeμ=v′μeμ′.v = v^\mu e_\mu = v'^\mu e'_\mu.v=vμeμ​=v′μeμ′​.

Substituting the basis transformation gives

v′μΛνμeν=vνeν,v'^\mu \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu = v^\nu e_\nu,v′μΛνμ​eν​=vνeν​,

hence

v′μ=(Λ−1)μνvν.v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.v′μ=(Λ−1)μν​vν.
note

In tensor language, the inverse of a matrix is a tensor satisfying

Λμρ(Λ−1)ρν=δμν.\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho (\Lambda^{-1})^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu.Λμρ​(Λ−1)ρν​=δμν​.

Dual Basis​

Starting from the definition of the dual basis,

δμν=eμ(eν),\delta^\mu{}_\nu = e^\mu(e_\nu),δμν​=eμ(eν​),

we multiply both sides by Λνρe′ρ\Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e'^\rhoΛνρ​e′ρ, obtaining

Λμρe′ρ=eμ(e′ρΛνρeν)=eμ(e′ρeρ′)=eμ.\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho e'^\rho = e^\mu(e'^\rho \Lambda^\nu{}_\rho e_\nu) = e^\mu(e'^\rho e'_\rho) = e^\mu.Λμρ​e′ρ=eμ(e′ρΛνρ​eν​)=eμ(e′ρeρ′​)=eμ.

Multiplying by (Λ−1)σμ(\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu(Λ−1)σμ​ yields

e′σ=(Λ−1)σμeμ.e'^\sigma = (\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu e^\mu.e′σ=(Λ−1)σμ​eμ.

Renaming dummy indices gives

e′μ=(Λ−1)μνeν.e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu.e′μ=(Λ−1)μν​eν.

Covector Components​

Similarly, starting from

ω=ωμeμ=ωμ′e′μ,\omega = \omega_\mu e^\mu = \omega'_\mu e'^\mu,ω=ωμ​eμ=ωμ′​e′μ,

and substituting the basis transformation, we obtain

ωμ′=Λνμων.\omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu.ωμ′​=Λνμ​ων​.

Contravariance and Covariance​

We now observe a unified pattern:

Contravariance and Covariance

The transformations of basis vectors and covector components are

eμ′=Λνμeν,ωμ′=Λνμων,e'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu, \qquad \omega'_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu \omega_\nu,eμ′​=Λνμ​eν​,ωμ′​=Λνμ​ων​,

while the transformations of dual basis vectors and vector components are

e′μ=(Λ−1)μνeν,v′μ=(Λ−1)μνvν.e'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu e^\nu, \qquad v'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu.e′μ=(Λ−1)μν​eν,v′μ=(Λ−1)μν​vν.

We see that any object carrying an upper index transforms with the inverse of the basis transformation, while any object with a lower index transforms with the transformation itself.

Thus, upper indices are called contravariant indices, and lower indices are called covariant indices; vectors are contravariant, and covectors are covariant (which is why dual vectors are also called covectors).

This explains why Einstein’s summation convention is defined in this way: indices do not merely indicate tensor type, but rather encode transformation behavior. Hence vectors use upper indices and covectors use lower indices, while repeated upper–lower pairs indicate summation, i.e. contraction, describing how tensors interact.

The reader may have heard of differential geometry, differentiable manifolds, etc. Let's now formally introduce the concept of a manifold.

2.2 Maps on Manifolds​

Unlike in topology, where we only talk about whether a map is continuous (C0C^0C0), the differentiable structure of a differentiable manifold allows us to talk about "nicer" maps, such as whether they are differentiable or even smooth.

Let MMM be an mmm-dimensional manifold and NNN an nnn-dimensional manifold, with atlases {(Oα,ψα)}\{(O_\alpha, \psi_\alpha)\}{(Oα​,ψα​)} and {(Oα′,ψα′)}\{(O'_\alpha, \psi'_\alpha)\}{(Oα′​,ψα′​)} respectively. note

Of course, different atlases (differentiable structures) may give different CrC^rCr classifications for the same map.

In particular, the notion of homeomorphism defined in topological spaces can be naturally extended to differentiable manifolds:

  1. fff is bijective;
  2. fff and f−1f^{-1}f−1 are both C∞C^\inftyC∞ maps,

note

Diffeomorphism is the strongest classification we can give so far regarding the relationship between two manifolds. Try to imagine: homeomorphism means you can deform a donut into a coffee cup with a square mouth (continuous), while diffeomorphism means you can only deform it into one with an elliptical mouth (smooth).

note

Scalar Fields​

If we consider the real numbers R\RR as a manifold, then a map from a manifold MMM to R\RR is a simple and important example.

Scalar Field

A map f:M→Rf: M \to \Rf:M→R is called a scalar field, or a function. Obviously, when MMM is the trivial manifold, a scalar field is just the familiar multivariate function.

In particular, we denote the set of all smooth functions on a manifold MMM by FM\mathscr{F}_MFM​, and omit the subscript when no confusion arises.

Let O⊂MO \subset MO⊂M be an open set. By the definition of a manifold, there exists a coordinate system (O,ψ)(O, \psi)(O,ψ). We can then define scalar fields:

Specifically, we can view xμx^\muxμ as the composition of two maps: xμ=projμ∘ψx^\mu = \text{proj}_\mu \circ \psixμ=projμ​∘ψ, where projμ\text{proj}_\muprojμ​ is the natural projection from the Cartesian product:

2.3 Tangent Vectors on Manifolds​

Motivation​

Before introducing tangent vectors on manifolds, let us consider a familiar example to aid understanding. Consider a smooth function f(x,y)f(x, y)f(x,y) defined on R2\R^2R2. At a point ppp, since fff is smooth, we can freely take derivatives. For example, the gradient:

∇f=(∂f∂x,∂f∂y).\nabla{f}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).∇f=(∂x∂f​,∂y∂f​).

Note that if we set z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y) in R3\R^3R3, we obtain a 2‑dimensional surface F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)−zF(x, y, z) = f(x, y) - zF(x,y,z)=f(x,y)−z. According to multivariable calculus, the 3‑dimensional gradient vector is the normal vector to the tangent plane of this surface:

∇F=(∂F∂x,∂F∂y,∂F∂z)=(∂f∂x,∂f∂y,−1).\nabla{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial z}\right) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, -1\right).∇F=(∂x∂F​,∂y∂F​,∂z∂F​)=(∂x∂f​,∂y∂f​,−1).

The surface is 2‑dimensional, hence its tangent plane is also 2‑dimensional; vectors in it have two degrees of freedom. From the normal vector we can determine the general form of tangent vectors:

(a,b,g(a,b))⋅(∂f∂x,∂f∂y,−1)=0⟹T=(a,b,a∂f∂x+b∂f∂y),\left(a, b, g(a,b)\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, -1\right) = 0 \Longrightarrow T = \left(a, b, a\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + b \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right),(a,b,g(a,b))⋅(∂x∂f​,∂y∂f​,−1)=0⟹T=(a,b,a∂x∂f​+b∂y∂f​),

from which we can obviously extract two basis vectors:

rx=(1,0,∂f∂x), ry=(0,1,∂f∂y).r_x = \left(1, 0, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right), \ r_y = \left(0, 1, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).rx​=(1,0,∂x∂f​), ry​=(0,1,∂y∂f​).

If you are not familiar with this conclusion, try proving it yourself; consider only the origin, compute the tangent curves in the xxx‑ and yyy‑directions; these two curves determine the tangent plane, and you can write down the tangent vectors in the plane. The result is the same as here.

Notice that this definition of the tangent plane does not actually depend on the choice of the function: for any smooth function, we can perform the above operation and the two basis vectors obtained have the same form. In other words, this suggests establishing a correspondence:

rx→∂∂x, ry→∂∂y.r_x \to \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \ r_y \to \frac{\partial}{\partial y}.rx​→∂x∂​, ry​→∂y∂​.
note

You might think this is completely unjustified, but it is in fact a very elegant shortcut: it is a very special isomorphism – an isomorphism between the geometric tangent plane and the space of (first‑order) directional derivatives (a very small subspace of a function space). Of course, for finite‑dimensional vector spaces, as long as the dimensions are the same, they are always isomorphic because they have the same number of basis vectors. However, the isomorphism here is actually independent of the choice of basis; the appearance of a basis here is only because we describe the surface using a parametrisation. It can be shown that for any parametrisation the difference is only a Jacobian matrix.

note

If you define the tangent plane using equivalence classes of curves through ppp, then you can completely eliminate the choice of basis and obtain a natural isomorphism. However, such an introduction requires some background in classical differential geometry and a certain familiarity with the language of abstract algebra, so we will not go into further detail here.

Another illuminating viewpoint: you can think of the tangent plane as the best linear approximation to the surface at that point. This perspective is quite helpful for understanding Lie group theory.

We shall see that for a general manifold it is difficult to find a geometric tangent plane, but given a smooth scalar field we can always find this space of derivatives, and thus naturally extend the concept to general manifolds.

note

When we only consider problems in R3\R^3R3, the tangent plane is not actually a vector (sub)space, but rather an element of a quotient space – a coset (which can be understood as a “translation” of a subspace). Therefore speaking of a “basis” of the tangent plane is actually imprecise. Formally, when we write two 3‑dimensional vectors as a basis for a 2‑dimensional plane, we are shifting the tangent plane back to the origin.

Of course, these issues arise because we have “embedded” the surface in a 3‑dimensional Euclidean space, imposing a global coordinate system. This also explains why the tangent vectors we computed earlier take such a special, simple form: we chose the simplest embedding z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y), which directly assigns the function value as the height over R2\R^2R2, and naturally obtain the simplest basis, namely (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (1,0)(1,0)(1,0).

On a manifold, coordinates are local and the atlas is always maximal, so it is legitimate to call a tangent plane a vector space.

note

There is another way to understand vectors: purely consider the “differential” of a function, and then take its functional to obtain a vector. That approach is much clearer, but the price is that it requires very rigorous mathematics – a lot of abstract algebra and more rigorous analysis. We do not have sufficient mathematical background to adopt that approach. Curious readers may refer to Chern’s Lectures on Differential Geometry.

Tangent Vector​

Inspired by the initial example, we now define a tangent vector at a point on a manifold:

Tangent Vector

Let v:FM→Rv: \mathscr{F}_M \to \Rv:FM​→R be a map defined at a point ppp on the manifold MMM satisfying:

  1. v(αf+βg)=αv(f)+βv(g),∀α,β∈Rv(\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha v(f) + \beta v(g), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in \Rv(αf+βg)=αv(f)+βv(g),∀α,β∈R (linearity, homogeneity)
  2. v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+v(f)g(p)v(fg) = f(p)v(g) + v(f) g(p)v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+v(f)g(p) (Leibniz rule)

Then vvv is called a tangent vector at ppp. The first property is naturally required for a vector, and the second property is required for a derivative. A map satisfying the second property is also called a derivation.

note

We emphasise here that the map is “defined at the point ppp”: a vector on a manifold is a local concept. Without further structure, we cannot compare vectors at two different points.

In Rn\R^nRn we can perform such a comparison because Rn\R^nRn has a natural translation that relates vectors at different points – Rn\R^nRn is flat.

note

Some differential geometry textbooks, especially Liang Canbin’s Differential Geometry and General Relativity, like to directly call tangent vectors “vectors”. This is not very precise because in the context of fibre bundles, there are many other vectors on a manifold besides tangent vectors, for example vector bundles of Hilbert spaces over a parameter manifold. We will discuss this in detail later.

Tangent Space​

The set of all tangent vectors at a point ppp on a manifold MMM naturally forms a vector space, called the tangent space and denoted TpMT_pMTp​M. According to our geometric intuition, the dimension of this tangent space should equal the dimension of the manifold. To verify this conjecture, we need to find a basis. And as suggested by the initial example, a natural choice is to consider partial derivatives. Note: once a coordinate system is given, any smooth scalar field fff corresponds to an nnn-variable function: F=f∘ψα−1=F(x1,x2,…,xn)F = f \circ \psi_\alpha^{-1} = F(x^1, x^2, \dots, x^n)F=f∘ψα−1​=F(x1,x2,…,xn). Therefore we take the basis vectors as:

Xμ:FM→R,Xμ(f)=∂F∂xμ∣p,μ=1,2,…,n,X_\mu: \mathscr{F}_M \to \R, \quad X_\mu(f) = \left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial x^\mu}\right|_{p}, \quad \mu = 1, 2, \dots, n,Xμ​:FM​→R,Xμ​(f)=∂xμ∂F​​p​,μ=1,2,…,n,

often abbreviated as ∂μ(f)=∂f∂xμ∣p\partial_\mu(f)=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\mu}\right|_{p}∂μ​(f)=∂xμ∂f​​p​. We call this basis the coordinate basis.

  1. Linear independence of {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​}: i.e., if there exist {αμ}\{\alpha^\mu\}{αμ} such that αμ∂μ=0\alpha^\mu \partial_\mu = 0αμ∂μ​=0, then αμ=0\alpha^\mu = 0αμ=0 for all μ\muμ;

  2. Completeness of {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​}: i.e., for any vector vvv, it can be expanded as a linear combination of {∂μ}\{\partial_\mu\}{∂μ​}: v=vμ∂μv = v^\mu \partial_\muv=vμ∂μ​, where vμ=v(xμ)v^\mu = v(x^\mu)vμ=v(xμ) are the components of vvv in the coordinate basis.

As before, we omit the proofs. The proof of linear independence is obvious: use the coordinate functions xμx^\muxμ themselves. The proof of completeness is long and not straightforward; we only sketch the idea: expand the nnn-variable function corresponding to a scalar field in a Taylor series up to first order, keeping an integral remainder (or some other form of remainder), and then show that any derivation (tangent vector) acting on the zeroth‑order term and the remainder yields zero.
